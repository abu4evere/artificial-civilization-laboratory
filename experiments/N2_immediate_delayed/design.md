# N2 — Immediate-return or delayed-return? (design document, not frozen)

**Written:** October 2026, at the creator's request, after N1's evidence review. **No code, no experiment, no choice.**

## Why

N1's evidence review found one source that changes the interpretation of the last ten experiments. Woodburn (1982)
distinguished **immediate-return** hunter-gatherer societies, where food is eaten soon after it is got, shared widely,
and little is stored, from **delayed-return** societies, with stores and lasting obligations. Immediate-return people
have full human bodies, appetites and foresight.

Before this, ACL's working story was: *people fail to store, so something in their nature is wrong.* The creator's
reframing: *people fail to store, and that may be a normal, stable human mode of life.* Then the question is no longer
"why don't they prepare?" but **"why would they ever prepare?"**, which is harder, and the right question to ask before
any change to nature.

## Sources and how far they can be trusted

- **Woodburn, J. (1982).** Egalitarian societies. *Man* 17(3). [doi:10.2307/2801707](https://doi.org/10.2307/2801707).
  **The paper itself was not read** (its publisher's page was closed to automated access, and no abstract is indexed).
  What follows about it is as the distinction is described in the literature that cites it, including work on the
  Nayaka of South India that uses Woodburn's terms. Every characteristic below attributed to Woodburn must be checked
  against the paper before anything is frozen.
- **Peterson, N. (1993).** Demand sharing: reciprocity and the pressure for generosity among foragers. *American
  Anthropologist* 95(4). [doi:10.1525/aa.1993.95.4.02a00050](https://doi.org/10.1525/aa.1993.95.4.02a00050) (abstract
  read). Despite an ethic of generosity, much forager sharing is **by demand**, and demand sharing helps constitute
  social relations in egalitarian societies.
- **Schnegg, M. (2015).** Reciprocity on demand. *Human Nature* 26(3).
  [doi:10.1007/s12110-015-9236-5](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12110-015-9236-5) (abstract read). In northwestern Namibia,
  people may demand food from anyone, yet the food-transfer networks that actually form are **highly reciprocal**.
- **Kelly, R. L. (2013).** *The Lifeways of Hunter-Gatherers.* Cambridge University Press.
  [doi:10.1017/cbo9781139176132](https://doi.org/10.1017/cbo9781139176132) (description read). Hunter-gatherers are
  **diverse**; foragers should not be used to depict "human nature stripped to its core". Variation is to be explained
  by behavioural ecology.
- **Binford, L. R. (1980)** and **Testart, A. (1982)**, already in the book: foragers who move to food and collectors
  who store, tied to seasonal environments (Binford); storage linked to settled life, dense populations and inequality
  (Testart).
- **Marean, C. W. (2016).** The transition to foraging for dense and predictable resources and its impact on the
  evolution of modern humans. *Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B.* [doi:10.1098/rstb.2015.0239](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2015.0239)
  (abstract read). Dense, predictable resources make land worth defending (economic defendability), raising
  territoriality and conflict.
- **Bowles, S. and Choi, J.-K. (2018).** The Neolithic agricultural revolution and the origins of private property.
  *Journal of Political Economy* 127(5). [doi:10.1086/701789](https://doi.org/10.1086/701789) (abstract read). Farming
  would be an unlikely choice without possession-based private property, which existed among rare **sedentary**
  hunter-gatherers who became the first farmers.
- **Wengrow, D. and Graeber, D. (2015).** Farewell to the 'childhood of man': ritual, seasonality, and the origins of
  inequality. *Journal of the Royal Anthropological Institute* 21(3).
  [doi:10.1111/1467-9655.12247](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9655.12247) (abstract read). Ice Age hunter-gatherers could
  **alternate deliberately between social modes**, an alternation that emerged in highly seasonal environments.

**Two cautions from the sources themselves.** Kelly warns against treating any forager society as the original human
condition. And Testart's and Binford's storing foragers show that many hunter-gatherers *did* store. Immediate-return is
**one** human mode, not *the* original one.

## 1. What characterises immediate-return societies?

As described in the literature citing Woodburn, and in Peterson and Schnegg (to be checked against Woodburn's paper):

1. Food is consumed soon after it is acquired; little is stored.
2. Food is shared widely, much of it on demand.
3. People have direct access to resources and are not bound to particular others for them; group membership is
   flexible and people can move away.
4. Few long-term binding commitments or obligations over goods.
5. Accumulation and standing are levelled (egalitarian).
6. Tools are simple, portable and quickly made.

Delayed-return societies have the opposite on several of these: stores, investment in facilities that pay later, and
lasting obligations that bind people to one another and to assets.

## 2. Which of these already exist in ACL? (from ACL's own record)

| characteristic | in ACL? | evidence |
|---|---|---|
| consumed soon after acquired, little stored | **yes** | W1: winter gathering covers 102% of winter eating, stock at its start 14%; U1: people gather about what they eat in every season |
| shared widely | **partly** | G1: given food goes 72% to family, 1.4% to strangers: shared within kin, not camp-wide |
| sharing on demand | **no** | ACL people cannot ask (README, "Not yet"); food is given when the giver sees hunger, never demanded |
| reciprocal in practice | **yes** | G1: 53% of food given is repayment, as Schnegg found under a demand norm |
| direct access, flexible membership | **mostly yes** | anyone can forage anywhere; no land is owned |
| few binding commitments | **yes, by construction** | ACL people cannot promise (README, "Not yet") |
| levelling | **unknown** | G1: a slight tilt of gifts toward the respected; levelling has not been measured |
| simple, portable tools | **yes** | E7, E8: making is rare, tools add little |

## 3. Which delayed-return features are missing?

- **Stores that persist:** the mechanism exists (stashes, seed that keeps), the practice does not (W1, S1).
- **Investment that pays later:** sowing exists (seed put in the ground: W1 counted 14,217 units in winter alone), but
  its returns and how people learn from them have not been studied as delayed return.
- **Binding commitments:** **cannot exist**. ACL people cannot ask or promise.
- **Possession of stores:** a stash has an owner, but anyone may take it; nothing defends it (in practice none was ever
  found gone, S1).
- **Settled life:** not measured as such; people move about 1.2 tiles a week (U1).

## 4. What moves real humans from one mode to the other?

From the sources above, the causes proposed are of several kinds:
- **The environment:** seasonality (Binford; Wengrow and Graeber), and resources that are **dense and predictable**
  (Marean), which make storing and defending worthwhile.
- **Settled life and population density** (Testart; Bowles and Choi).
- **Property:** possession-based private property that makes holding and investing safe (Bowles and Choi).
- **Social commitments and obligations** that bind people to one another over time (the delayed-return side of
  Woodburn's distinction, as described).
- And Wengrow and Graeber add that people may move **between** modes, seasonally and deliberately.

## 5. Which causes are nature, and which are culture?

| cause | nature or culture in ACL's terms | in ACL now |
|---|---|---|
| seasonality | **nature of the world** (physics) | present: four seasons, a hard winter |
| dense, predictable resources | **nature of the world** | **open question**: wild food is spread thinly over many tiles (64 a tile at most, regrowing slowly); whether ACL has any dense, predictable, storable pulse (seed in autumn is 35% of a harvest and keeps) has not been measured |
| settled life, density | culture (a way of living), shaped by the world | not measured |
| property and possession | **culture** (norms about who may take what) | none; anyone may take a stash |
| the capacity to make commitments (ask, promise) | **nature** (a capacity), whose content is culture | **absent**, by design so far |
| storage technology (containers, covered heaps) | discovered, so culture, on physics that is nature | covered heaps exist; watertight vessels never appeared (E7.6) |

## What this changes

1. **U1 identified a candidate bottleneck, not a proven flaw.** The two-week stop is real, but the human record shows
   people with full foresight who do not store, because of how they live together. ACL may have converged on an
   immediate-return mode of life, which is a finding in itself.
2. **The question shifts** from "what should satiety be?" (N1) to **"under what conditions do delayed-return societies
   emerge, and does ACL's world contain them?"**
3. **The N1 candidates are not discarded,** but each becomes a hypothesis to weigh against this one. A change to
   satiety that produces storage could mean either that satiety was wrong, or that an immediate-return society was
   pushed into a delayed-return one by hand. Those are different conclusions, and a design would have to tell them
   apart.
4. **Two things in the table are capacities of nature, not culture, and are absent:** dense, predictable resources (if
   the world lacks them), and the capacity to make commitments. Neither tells anyone to store. Both are named in the
   literature as conditions for delayed return. They are candidates of a different kind from N1's, and belong beside
   them.

## Read-only questions this suggests (not scheduled; the creator decides)

- **Does ACL's world offer dense, predictable, storable resources?** Map, from the existing worlds, where and when food
  is concentrated (seed in autumn, crops from sowing, hunts), and how predictable it is year to year.
- **Is sowing a delayed return that people learn?** How often seed is sown, what it yields and when, and whether sowers
  learn that it paid.
- **Is there levelling?** Do people who hold more give more, and do they lose standing for holding?
