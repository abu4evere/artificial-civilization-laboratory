# N5 — Evidence: how real foragers find partners

**Written:** October 2026, after F1 and the reopening of stage 0 by the creator. Questions: `questions.md` (committed
before any search). **No code, no experiment, no choice.** "Abstract read" means the abstract was read in full (OpenAlex
or the publisher); "summary read" means the full text was read through a page summary tool (numbers quoted as reported
there); "record only" means the bibliographic record and secondary reports, not the work itself.

## Sources

**How large the pool is**
- **Wobst, H. M. (1974).** Boundary conditions for Paleolithic social systems: a simulation approach. *American
  Antiquity* 39: 147–178 (**record only**, as reported by White 2017 and Pearce 2014). A minimum equilibrium size of a
  mating network of 79–332 persons across model runs; 175–475 with spatial assumptions.
- **White, A. (2017).** A model-based analysis of the minimum size of demographically-viable hunter-gatherer
  populations. *JASSS* 20(4): 9 ([jasss.org/20/4/9.html](https://www.jasss.org/20/4/9.html); **summary read**). An
  agent-based model, non-spatial (any eligible partner can be found): the population surviving 400 years in 95% of runs
  ranged from **40** persons (polygyny, no incest taboo) to **140** (monogamy, incest taboo, eight marriage divisions);
  "about 150 persons is demographically viable", "as few as 75 persons have a good chance". Female age at marriage is the
  key variable. The non-spatial model gives lower sizes than Wobst's spatial one: space raises the threshold.
- **Pearce, E. (2014).** Modelling mechanisms of social network maintenance in hunter-gatherers. *Journal of
  Archaeological Science* 50: 403–413 ([PMC4157219](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4157219/); **summary
  read**). Network layers of about 5, 15, 50, 150, **500 (the mating pool)** and 1,500. A model of encounters from random
  movement: at low densities (above about 40° latitude) "random mobility no longer produces a sufficient number of
  encounters between groups to maintain contact across the 500-strong mating pool"; foragers keep contact by
  **periodic aggregations** (used to broker marriages), returns to home bases, exchange of objects and extended kin terms.
- **Hill, K. R., Wood, B. M., Baggio, J., Hurtado, A. M. and Boyd, R. T. (2014).** Hunter-gatherer inter-band
  interaction rates. *PLOS ONE* 9(7): e102806 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0102806); **summary read**).
  Hadza and Ache adults interact with **more than 300 same-sex adults** in a lifetime, "a social universe of about a
  thousand individuals"; yearly interaction probability between random same-sex adults 5–29%. Does not discuss spouses.

**Who lives together, and who moves**
- **Hill, K. R., Walker, R. S., Božičević, M. et al. (2011).** Co-residence patterns in hunter-gatherer societies show
  unique human social structure. *Science* 331: 1286–1289
  ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1199071); **abstract read**). 32 foraging societies, 5,067 individuals, mean
  band 28.2 adults; **either sex may disperse or stay**; most co-residents are unrelated (primary kin under 10%).
- **Dyble, M. et al. (2015).** Sex equality can explain the unique social structure of hunter-gatherer bands.
  *Science* 348: 796–798 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaa5139); **abstract read**). Low within-camp
  relatedness and **fluid meta-groups**; a model in which men and women both choose camp members reproduces Agta and
  BaYaka co-residence.
- **Kramer, K. L., Schacht, R. and Bell, A. V. (2017).** Adult sex ratios and partner scarcity among hunter-gatherers.
  *Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B* 372: 20160316 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2016.0316); **abstract read**). 25 years
  of Savanna Pumé data: in small groups the adult sex ratio **fluctuates substantially from year to year**, driven by
  chance in the sex ratio at maturity; **dispersal outside the group** is the main way people meet locally constrained
  mating options; "in small populations, looking outside of one's local group is necessary to find a mate".
- **Marlowe, F. W. (2010)**, *The Hadza* (**record only**, as reported in secondary sources): camps of about 30, moving
  about every six weeks; individuals moved camp 6.5–9 times a year (a later study reports far fewer moves today).
- **Smith, K. M., Larroucau, T., Mabulla, I. A. and Apicella, C. L. (2018).** Hunter-gatherers maintain assortativity
  in cooperation despite high levels of residential change and mixing. *Current Biology* 28
  ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2018.07.064); **record only**): its title reports high residential change among
  the Hadza; numbers not read.

**How marriages are made**
- **Walker, R. S., Hill, K. R., Flinn, M. V. and Ellsworth, R. M. (2011).** Evolutionary history of hunter-gatherer
  marriage practices. *PLOS ONE* 6(4): e19066 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0019066); **summary read**).
  Marriage **arranged by parents or close kin** is the primary mode in 85% of a comparative sample and 67% of their
  hunter-gatherer sample; **bride service, bride price or exchange between families** in 80%; under 20% of men
  polygynous in 87% of societies; arranged marriage reconstructed back at least 50,000 years; regulated marriage built
  alliances "spanning across multiple residential communities".
- **MacDonald, D. H. and Hewlett, B. S. (1999).** Reproductive interests and forager mobility. *Current Anthropology*
  40: 501–524 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1086/200047); **abstract read**). Mating distance **correlates with population
  density** (lower density, farther); in every culture with data, **men travelled considerably farther than women** in a
  lifetime; reproductive interests shape mobility at every level. (Distances themselves not read.)
- **Yellen, J. and Harpending, H. (1972)** (**record only**, as cited): the minimum equilibrium size of a population is
  the number of people living in the distance between two marriage partners.

## Answers to the questions

1. **Pool size.** Real foragers find spouses in networks of hundreds: a mating pool of about 500 (Pearce), with a
   minimum viable population of about 150, and perhaps 75, even when any partner can be found at once (White);
   higher once space is modelled (Wobst 175–475). Adults meet over 300 same-sex adults in a lifetime (Hill 2014).
2. **Distance.** Farther at lower density, and farther for men (MacDonald and Hewlett). No distance can be carried into
   ACL, which has no physical scale (`questions.md`).
3. **By what means.** Not by chance encounters within sight. By **looking outside the local group** (Kramer et al.), by
   **fluid camps** that people join and leave many times a year (Hill 2011, Dyble, Marlowe), by **periodic
   aggregations** where marriages are brokered (Pearce), and by **arrangement through kin and exchange between
   families** (Walker et al.). Random movement alone fails at low density (Pearce's model).
4. **Who chooses.** Usually parents or close kin (67–85%), with exchange between families.
5. **When local partners are lacking.** Sex ratios swing by chance in small groups, and people disperse outward to find
   partners; the rarer sex moves (Kramer et al.).
6. **How small can a people be.** Under about 75–150 (non-spatial), more with space, the risk of dying out rises sharply.
7. **Residence.** Either sex may move; bands are mostly unrelated people; membership is fluid (Hill 2011, Dyble).

| movement between groups | constant | no mechanism beyond wandering |

Two gaps, distinct:

- **Size.** ACL's entire world is smaller than every estimate of a viable forager population, and every estimate assumes
  partners can be found. Even with perfect search, a monogamous people with a kin rule like ACL's needs well over 40
  people (White). **Rule 12 applies:** before any change to search, check read-only whether 20 founders could persist
  even if everyone could find any eligible partner.
- **Search.** ACL's search is the random mobility that Pearce's model shows fails at low density.

## Nature or culture? (the founding rule)

Most of the real means are **cultural**: arranged marriage, bride service, exchange between families, aggregation
rituals, extended kin terms. ACL may not write these (rule 1); if they appear, nobody may have scripted them. What is
**nature** in the evidence: a drive to seek a partner; the capacity to remember people met and where they live (Hill
2014's hundreds of contacts); to travel to them; to know of others through kin; and the size and density of the
population itself. Any change must stay on the nature side and be anchored there (rule 17).

## Assistant's view (the creator decides)

The evidence says ACL's people are trying to do something no real people do: find partners among a handful of others,
by chance, within sight, in a world that holds fewer people than any known viable network. The first step should be
**rule 12's feasibility check, read-only:** in ACL's present worlds, how often does an eligible partner exist somewhere
and stay unmet, and would ACL's 20 founders persist even with perfect search? If not, size (M1) comes first and search
second. Any change to search (M2, M3) must be anchored to the scale-free evidence above (people known, not tiles), and
its value fixed before any run, never widened until worlds survive (rule 17).
