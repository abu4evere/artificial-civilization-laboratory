# N9 — Evidence: how much do the unpartnered search?

**Written:** October 2026. Questions and anchor criteria: `questions.md` (tag `N9-questions`, committed before any
search). **No code in ACL, no experiment, no choice.** "Full text read" = text extracted and the relevant passages read;
"abstract read" = abstract read in full (OpenAlex, PubMed, Europe PMC or bioRxiv); "record only" = bibliographic record
or snippet. Several full texts (Smith et al. 2016; Wood et al. 2021; Dyble et al. 2019 on Agta time budgets; Sugawara
1988; Padilla-Iglesias et al. 2025) could not be retrieved from this machine (publisher blocks); for these only the
abstract was read.

## Sources

- **Hill, K. R., Wood, B. M., Baggio, J., Hurtado, A. M. and Boyd, R. T. (2014).** Hunter-gatherer inter-band
  interaction rates: implications for cumulative culture. *PLoS ONE* 9: e102806 (PMID 25047714;
  [doi](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0102806); **full text read**, relevant passages). Ache and Hadza: random
  same-sex adult pairs living in different bands have a 5–29% chance per year of a given kind of interaction; "because
  of visiting and migration, band composition was stable over periods of months but not years" (Ache); the Discussion
  speaks of "frequent visiting". Interactions "often result from targeted visits" but the paper gives **no figure for
  how often a person travels, or for what share of their time**.
- **Dyble, M., Migliano, A. B., Page, A. E. and Smith, D. (2021).** Relatedness within and between Agta residential
  groups. *Evolutionary Human Sciences* (PMID 37588565; [doi](https://doi.org/10.1017/ehs.2021.46); **full text
  read**, via Europe PMC). Camp stability (1 = no change in membership between the researchers' visits, 0 = complete
  turnover) ranged 0.12–0.79 across 11 camps, mean 0.51. The interval between visits is not given in the passages read,
  so the figure is **not a rate per week or year**.
- **Smith, D. et al. (2016).** Camp stability predicts patterns of hunter–gatherer cooperation. *Royal Society Open
  Science* (PMID 27493770; [doi](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.160131); **abstract read**). Agta camps vary
  greatly in membership change over time; the measure used by Dyble et al. 2021.
- **Wood, B. M., Raichlen, D. A., Pontzer, H. et al. (2024).** Beyond the here and now: hunter-gatherer socio-spatial
  complexity and the evolution of language. *Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B* (PMID 39230448;
  [doi](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2022.0521); **full text read**, via Europe PMC). Hadza: people leave camp in the
  morning and return in the evening; "a small subset of camp members might ... leave on short visits to neighbouring
  camps". **No frequency given.**
- **Pearce, E. (2014).** Modelling mechanisms of social network maintenance in hunter-gatherers. *Journal of
  Archaeological Science* (PMID 25214706; [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2014.08.004); **full text
  read**, via PubMed Central; carried from N5). Random movement does not produce enough encounters to keep the mating pool
  connected above about 40° latitude; behaviours that raise encounter rates (return to home bases, periodic aggregations)
  and cultural scaffolding are expected. States that "empirical data to validate these models is so far lacking", and
  that seasonal aggregations "are often used as opportunities to broker marriages" (an institution: culture).
- **Wood, B. M. et al. (2021).** Gendered movement ecology and landscape use in Hadza hunter-gatherers. *Nature Human
  Behaviour* ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-020-01002-7); **abstract read**). 2,078 GPS person-days:
  men walk further, explore more land and are more often alone than women. Daily foraging movement, not visiting.
- **Padilla-Iglesias, C. et al. (2025).** Sexual division of labour shapes hunter-gatherer spatial ranges. bioRxiv
  ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.04.15.649057); **abstract read**; preprint, not peer reviewed). 776 Mbendjele
  BaYaka: men's lifetime ranges expand from adolescence to adulthood, women's stay stable; men's mobility is driven by
  hunting and "exploratory trips away from camps"; larger ranges are associated with higher reproductive success in both
  sexes, which the authors suggest may reflect access to resources, networks "and potential mates". Correlational; no
  figure for time spent.
- **Sugawara, K. (1988).** Visiting relations and social interactions between residential groups of the Central Kalahari
  San. *African Study Monographs* 9 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.14989/68034); **abstract read**). Quantitative visiting
  records, but in a **settled** community (!Koi!kom, after 1979); women's visiting follows close kin, men's ranges wider.
  Not foraging conditions.
- **Carried from N5/N8:** Kramer et al. 2017 (dispersal outside the group when partners are locally scarce); Hill et al.
  2011 (either sex may disperse at marriage).

## Answers

1. **How often do forager adults visit other camps?** Often, by every account read ("frequent visiting"; band
   composition stable over months, not years; Agta camp membership turning over substantially between researcher visits;
   a subset of Hadza leave on short visits). **No source read gives a frequency per person or a share of time.**
2. **Do the unpartnered visit more, to find spouses?** **No source read compares unmarried with married adults.** Men's
   ranges grow from adolescence into adulthood and larger ranges go with more children (BaYaka; abstract, preprint,
   correlational); dispersal answers locally scarce partners (Kramer 2017, carried). The link to spouse search is
   suggested, not measured.
3. **Time budgets:** the time-allocation studies found (Agta, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-019-0614-6); eight small-scale societies, *PNAS* 2019,
   [doi](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1906196116)) were
   not readable in full here or do not separate visiting; **no share of time for travel to other camps was found.**
4. **Trade-off with food:** not addressed in what was read.
5. **Nature or culture:** fluid residence and visiting are described across foragers (Ache, Hadza, Agta, BaYaka, San),
   which favours a species-typical tendency; marriage brokering at aggregations, hxaro-type exchange partners and
   ritual sites are institutions (culture, not to be written).

## Against the anchor criteria (fixed before the search)

| criterion | verdict |
|---|---|
| two independent quantitative sources for how often adults (or the unpartnered) travel to other camps, convertible to ACL's terms without a further chosen number | **not met.** The figures found are of other things: yearly dyad interaction probabilities (Hill 2014) and camp-membership stability between unstated intervals (Dyble 2021). Converting either into "share of weeks a person travels" would need numbers chosen by us (band sizes, visit intervals) |
| one quantitative source, or several qualitative ones agreeing on direction | **met for direction only:** visiting and residential change are frequent, and men range more widely than women, across several societies |

**Verdict: partly anchored (direction only). No number can be set.** By the criteria written beforehand, **no number in
ACL is changed**: how much a single adult searches cannot be raised on this evidence. **P2 is closed in its present
form** (direction-only, which the pilot showed barely acts) unless a quantitative source is found later; this is recorded
as a limit of the evidence, not as a finding that foragers search little.

## What the evidence does suggest (leads, not choices)

- The best-attested pattern is not a lone single's search but **fluid residence**: people move between bands over
  months (Ache), and camp membership turns over (Agta). Whether ACL's groups are as fluid could be checked **read-only**
  in existing control runs (how much a group's membership changes over a year), against the qualitative benchmark
  "stable over months, not years". If ACL's groups are far more stable than this, that gap is anchored in direction,
  though not in size.
- Pearce (2014) names what raises encounter rates in real foragers: returns to home bases and periodic aggregations.
  Aggregation for marriage is an institution (culture); returns to a home base were HB1 (harmful in ACL). Neither is a
  new lever on this evidence.
- A quantitative visiting figure most likely exists in ethnographies not readable here (for example time-allocation
  tables in monographs); if the creator can supply one, the review can be reopened against the same criteria.
