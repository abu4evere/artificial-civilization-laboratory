# N1 — Human evidence behind the leading candidates (C3, C4, C8, C11)

**Written:** October 2026, at the creator's request, before any candidate is chosen. **No code, no experiment.**
Every source below was checked against its record: PubMed (abstract read) or Crossref (bibliographic record; content as
summarised in the literature, marked *record only* where the abstract was not read). Nothing here is from memory alone.

## C3 — Smooth satiety (each extra week of food worth less, never nothing)

- **Cabanac, M. (1971).** Physiological role of pleasure. *Science* 173(4002):1103–7.
  [doi:10.1126/science.173.4002.1103](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.173.4002.1103) (PubMed, abstract read).
  The same food is pleasant or unpleasant depending on the body's internal state ("alliesthesia"); the pull of food is
  graded by need, not switched off at a line.
- **Layard, R., Mayraz, G. and Nickell, S. (2008).** The marginal utility of income. *Journal of Public Economics*.
  [doi:10.1016/j.jpubeco.2008.01.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpubeco.2008.01.007) (record only). Each extra unit of
  a resource adds less to wellbeing than the last, smoothly.

**Assessment: moderate, and general.** Graded, diminishing value is well supported as a principle. But neither source
is about valuing a *stock of food held for the future*; Cabanac's is about the body's state, Layard's about income. C3
rests on a general principle, not on direct evidence about food stocks.

## C4 — Insecurity learned from one's own swings

- **Malmendier, U. and Nagel, S. (2011).** Depression babies: do macroeconomic experiences affect risk taking?
  *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 126(1). [doi:10.1093/qje/qjq004](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjq004) (record only).
  People's own lived experience of hard times durably shapes their later caution; recent experience weighs most.
- **Nettle, D., Andrews, C. and Bateson, M. (2016).** Food insecurity as a driver of obesity in humans: the insurance
  hypothesis. *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 40:e105.
  [doi:10.1017/S0140525X16000947](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X16000947) (PubMed, abstract read). Cues that access
  to food is uncertain should lead to storing more (as body fat). Robust in non-human animals; in humans, a meta-analysis
  of 125 studies finds a positive association, but **only in adult women in high-income countries**.
- **Shah, A. K., Mullainathan, S. and Shafir, E. (2012).** Some consequences of having too little. *Science*
  338(6107):682–5. [doi:10.1126/science.1222426](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1222426) (PubMed, abstract read).
  Scarcity **narrows attention to pressing needs** and leads to choices, like overborrowing, that sacrifice the future.

**Assessment: moderate, and mixed in direction.** That lived scarcity changes later behaviour is well supported. But
the direction is not settled: scarcity can make people more cautious and store more (Malmendier and Nagel; Nettle et al.
in animals), or it can narrow them to the present (Shah et al.). The human evidence for "insecurity makes people
store" is weaker than for animals.

## C8 — A longer mind (linking what one does now to what happens months later)

- **Suddendorf, T. and Corballis, M. C. (2007).** The evolution of foresight: what is mental time travel, and is it
  unique to humans? *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 30(3):299–313.
  [doi:10.1017/S0140525X07001975](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X07001975) (PubMed, abstract read). Humans can
  foresee and plan for specific future events; no convincing evidence of this in other animals. A crucial step in human
  evolution.
- **Frederick, S., Loewenstein, G. and O'Donoghue, T. (2002).** Time discounting and time preference: a critical review.
  *Journal of Economic Literature* 40(2):351. [doi:10.1257/jel.40.2.351](https://doi.org/10.1257/jel.40.2.351) (record
  only). People weigh the future, but often heavily discount it, and inconsistently.
- **Mischel, W., Shoda, Y. and Rodriguez, M. L. (1989).** Delay of gratification in children. *Science*.
  [doi:10.1126/science.2658056](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.2658056) (record only). The ability to wait for a later
  reward varies between people and develops with age.
- **Woodburn, J. (1982).** Egalitarian societies. *Man* 17. [doi:10.2307/2801707](https://doi.org/10.2307/2801707)
  (record only). Distinguishes **immediate-return** hunter-gatherer societies, where food is consumed soon after it is
  got and shared widely, with little storage, from **delayed-return** societies, with stores and lasting obligations.

**Assessment: strong for the capacity, weak for its use.** Humans clearly can think months ahead. But they discount the
future heavily, and whole human societies with that capacity, living by sharing, do not store much. **Foresight does not
by itself produce storage.**

## C11 — The body as a store

- **Wells, J. C. K. (2006).** The evolution of human fatness and susceptibility to obesity: an ethological approach.
  *Biological Reviews* 81(2):183–205. [doi:10.1017/S1464793105006974](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1464793105006974)
  (PubMed, abstract read). Human fat stores buffer malnutrition; humans are unusually fat for their environment, linked
  to uncertainty in energy supply, and early human evolution in a seasonal environment favoured thriftiness.
- **Simondon, K. B. et al. (2004).** Season of birth is not associated with risk of early adult death in rural Senegal.
  *International Journal of Epidemiology* 33(1):130–6. [doi:10.1093/ije/dyg279](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyg279)
  (PubMed, abstract read). Women's weight varied strongly by season: **3.0–3.9 kg lower** at the end of the rainy season
  than in the dry season, every year.
- **Nettle et al. (2016)**, above: storing fat as a buffer against shortfall is the function of fat, on their account.

**Assessment: strong.** Body reserves that fill in good seasons and drain in lean ones are well documented in humans,
including in seasonal subsistence populations.

## What the evidence adds that the candidates document did not

**Not storing is a normal human pattern.** Woodburn's immediate-return societies are, in their sharing and their
consumption of food soon after it is got, close to what G1 and W1 found in ACL: food shared widely inside families, to
the hungry, and eaten rather than kept. In those human societies, people have full human foresight and still store
little; storage belongs to a different organisation of society, which is culture. This suggests ACL's people may not
have broken nature at all, and that what is missing could be culture rather than a capacity. **It is an argument for
taking C0 (change nothing in nature) seriously**, and it must be weighed before any change.
