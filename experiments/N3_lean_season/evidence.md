# N3 — Evidence: what do real lean seasons look like?

**Written:** October 2026, after D2, as the creator's next priority. **No code, no experiment, no choice.** Every source
below was checked against its record; "abstract read" means its abstract was read in full (via OpenAlex or the

## Sources

**Plants in a temperate winter**
- **Polgar, C. A. and Primack, R. B. (2011).** Leaf-out phenology of temperate woody plants: from trees to ecosystems.
  *New Phytologist.* [doi:10.1111/j.1469-8137.2011.03803.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-8137.2011.03803.x) (abstract
  read). Leaf-out begins the growing season in temperate forests and is highly sensitive to temperature.
- **Vitasse, Y., Lenz, A. and Körner, C. (2014).** The interaction between freezing tolerance and phenology in temperate
  deciduous trees. *Frontiers in Plant Science.* [doi:10.3389/fpls.2014.00541](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2014.00541)
  (abstract read). Temperate climates have distinct temperature seasonality; trees acclimate in autumn and go dormant
  through winter.
- **Bowling, D. R. et al. (2024).** Phenology of photosynthesis in winter-dormant temperate and boreal forests.
  *JGR Biogeosciences.* [doi:10.1029/2023jg007839](https://doi.org/10.1029/2023jg007839) (abstract read). Photosynthesis
  in such forests stops in winter and restarts in spring.

**Snow and access to food (animals only)**
- **Korslund, L. and Steen, H. (2005).** Small rodent winter survival: snow conditions limit access to food resources.
  *Journal of Animal Ecology.* [doi:10.1111/j.1365-2656.2005.01031.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2656.2005.01031.x)
  (abstract read). Ice and snow break food that is present into accessible and inaccessible patches.
- **Rixen, C. et al. (2022).** Winters are changing: snow effects on Arctic and alpine tundra ecosystems. *Arctic
  Science.* [doi:10.1139/as-2020-0058](https://doi.org/10.1139/as-2020-0058) (abstract read). Snow governs ground
  conditions, the growing season's start and end, and plants' access to water and nutrients.

**What lean seasons do to human foragers**
- **Zhu, D. et al. (2021).** Global hunter-gatherer population densities constrained by influence of seasonality on diet
  composition. *Nature Ecology & Evolution.* [doi:10.1038/s41559-021-01548-3](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41559-021-01548-3)
  (abstract read on the publisher's page). Where growing seasons are short, hunter-gatherers eat a much higher share of
  meat, and their populations are far less dense: a "seasonal carnivory bottleneck".
- **Tallavaara, M. et al. (2017).** Productivity, biodiversity, and pathogens influence the global hunter-gatherer
  population density. *PNAS.* [doi:10.1073/pnas.1715638115](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1715638115) (abstract read).
  Productivity drives hunter-gatherer population density globally.
- **Simondon, K. B. et al. (2004)** (already in N1): in rural Senegal, women's weight falls 3.0–3.9 kg in the lean season
  every year. People also carry lean seasons in their bodies.

**When foragers store, own and rank**
- **Watts, J. et al. (2022).** Food storage facilitates professional religious specialization in hunter–gatherer
  societies. *Evolutionary Human Sciences.* [doi:10.1017/ehs.2022.17](https://doi.org/10.1017/ehs.2022.17) (abstract
  read). Across a global sample of hunter-gatherers, food storage is correlated with **environmental predictability** and
  **richness**, among other factors.
- **Smith, E. A. and Codding, B. F. (2021).** Ecological variation and institutionalized inequality in hunter-gatherer
  societies. *PNAS.* [doi:10.1073/pnas.2016134118](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2016134118) (abstract read). Among 89
  Pacific-coast societies, inequality is best predicted by **reliance on and ownership of clumped** resources (mainly
  salmon) rather than wild plants; population pressure barely matters.
- **Binford (1980)** and **Testart (1982)** (already in the book; record only): storing collectors in seasonal
  environments; storage with settled life, density and inequality.

## What the evidence says, candidate by candidate

### L1 — No growth in winter
**Supported, strongly.** In temperate and boreal climates, plants go dormant in winter and restart growth with spring
warmth (Polgar and Primack; Vitasse et al.; Bowling et al.). ACL's current winter, in which plants still regrow at 15% of
their spring rate, is not what a temperate winter looks like. Zero is not a dial but the realistic value.

### L2 — Most plant food gone in winter
**Partly supported, and with a twist.** Short growing seasons do leave human foragers short of plant food: they turn to
**meat** (Zhu et al.). The evidence supports a winter in which plant food is scarce; it does not give a number for how
fast it disappears, so L2's dieback rate stays a dial. And it says the realistic human response to a plant-poor winter is
**hunting**, not only storing. In ACL hunting is almost nothing (W1: 274 units hunted in winter against 4.2 million
foraged), so an ACL with a harsher L2 winter would lack the main human alternative.

### L3 — Food present but out of reach under snow
**Supported in animals, not shown here for humans.** Snow and ice cut animals off from food that is still there
(Korslund and Steen; Rixen et al.). No human source was found in this review.

### L6 — Leaner winters where it is colder
**Supported, strongly.** Growing seasons vary with climate, and that variation shapes how foragers eat and how densely
they live (Zhu et al.; Tallavaara et al.). A world whose colder places have a real lean season and whose warmer places do
not is the realistic case.

## What the evidence adds that N3's candidates did not

1. **A lean season has several human answers, not one.** Foragers meet short growing seasons by eating more meat (Zhu),
   by losing body weight (Simondon), by moving and switching ways of living (Wengrow and Graeber, in N2), and by storing
   (Binford, Testart, Watts). An ACL experiment with a harder winter must be able to conclude that people survived it
   some other way than by storing, and must count that as a finding, not a failure.
2. **Storage goes with predictability, richness and clumped, owned resources.** ACL is already predictable (D1: tiles
   correlate 0.90 year to year) and rich in total, but its food is thin and nothing is owned (D1, D2). The storing and
   ranked societies in Smith and Codding relied on clumped resources they owned. A harder winter alone may not be enough
   if food stays thin and unowned. That links N3 to the property question, under the creator's rule for it.
3. **ACL's people have one of the human answers already, and lack two.** They have sharing (G1, P1) and could store
   (stashes and seed). They lack effective hunting (W1) and body reserves (N1's C11). A harsher winter would test the
   answers ACL has.

## The candidates, against the evidence

| | candidate | evidence | note |
|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | no growth in winter | **strong** (temperate dormancy) | realistic; zero is principled; may change little by arithmetic (N3) |
| L2 | strong winter dieback | partial (plant-poor short seasons; no rate) | the dieback rate stays a dial; real people would hunt |
| L3 | snow: food out of reach | animals only | no human source found |
| L6 | colder places, leaner winters | **strong** | the realistic case; the largest change |

## Assistant's view (the creator decides)

The evidence supports **L1** (its zero is the realistic value, not a choice) and **L6** (the realistic shape of a world).
It weakens L2's claim to realism (its rate is not given by any source) and leaves L3 resting on animals. The creator's
order stands: an **L1 pilot** is supported by the evidence. Its design must be ready for two honest outcomes: that L1
changes little (as N3's arithmetic suggests), and that people meet a leaner winter by some other means than storing.
