# N6 — Evidence: how real forager couples live together

**Written:** October 2026, after C1. Questions: `questions.md` (committed before any search). **No code, no experiment, no
choice.** "Abstract read" means the abstract was read in full (OpenAlex or the publisher); "record only" means the
bibliographic record and secondary reports, not the work itself.

## Sources

**The camp as a central place**
- **Garg, K., Padilla-Iglesias, C., Restrepo Ochoa, N., Knight, V. B. et al. (2021).** Hunter-gatherer foraging
  networks promote information transmission. *Royal Society Open Science* 8: 211324
  ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.211324); **abstract read**). Central-place foraging, "where foragers return to a
  central location (or home), is a key feature of hunter-gatherer social organization"; foragers either forage close to
  home within a radius or move the home to a new area.
- **Kelly, R. L. (2013).** *The Lifeways of Hunter-Gatherers: The Foraging Spectrum.* Cambridge University Press
  (**record only**, with secondary summaries): two kinds of mobility, daily trips out from camp and back before dark,
  and moves of the residential camp itself; foraging radius against camp moves.
- **Marlowe, F. W. (2010).** *The Hadza* (**record only**, as reported in secondary sources; carried from N5): camps of
  about 30 that move about every six weeks.

**Spouses by day: the division of labour**
- **Marlowe, F. W. (2007).** Hunting and gathering: the human sexual division of foraging labor. *Cross-Cultural
  Research* 41(2) ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/1069397106297529); **abstract read**). Men and women target different
  foods and **share them**; the division of labour is weaker in less seasonal, more productive habitats; it "likely
  evolved after pair bonds".
- **Hewlett, B. S.** Husband-wife reciprocity and the father-infant relationship among Aka pygmies (book chapter; 2017
  reprint, [doi](https://doi.org/10.4324/9780203792063-15); **abstract read**). Aka fathers are within arm's reach of
  their infant for more than half of a 24-hour day, with far more care in camp than out on the net hunt. (A further
  figure, that Aka spouses are **within view of each other 47% of the time on net-hunting days**, is **record only**,
  from secondary reports of Hewlett's work.)

**Who lives with whom** (carried from N5)
- **Hill, K. R. et al. (2011).** *Science* 331 (**abstract read**): residential groups of foragers (mean 28 adults) in
  which either sex may disperse at marriage; married couples are counted as co-residents of a band.
- **Dyble, M. et al. (2015).** *Science* 348 (**abstract read**): men and women both choose where to live; camps are
  fluid.

**Separation and births**
- **Potter, R. G., Abowitz, D. A. and Kobrin, F. E. (1983).** Fertility effects of isolated spouse separations in
  relation to their timing. *Social Biology* ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/19485565.1983.9988542); **abstract
  read**). Under natural fertility, a separation's effect on births depends on its length, how completely it removes the
  chance of conception, and when it starts relative to the wife's reproductive state.
- **Nie, W. (2020).** The effect of spousal separation and reunification on fertility: Chinese internal and
  international migration. *Demographic Research* 43(29) ([doi](https://doi.org/10.4054/DemRes.2020.43.29); **abstract
  read**, opening only): spousal separation has "a substantial and cumulative effect on marital fertility", citing
  Menken (1979) (**record only**).

## Answers to the questions

1. **Co-residence.** Forager spouses live in the same camp (Hill 2011 counts them as co-residents of a band; Dyble
   2015: both choose the camp). No source read here gives a share of nights spouses spend in different camps; longer
   separations exist (men travel farther over a lifetime: MacDonald and Hewlett 1999, in N5) but are not the norm.
2. **Daily foraging.** Spouses often forage **apart by day** (the sexual division of labour, Marlowe 2007), less so in
   productive, less seasonal habitats, and in some societies together (Aka net hunts, where spouses are within view of
   each other about half the time: record only).
3. **The camp as a unit.** Foragers return to a **central place** at the end of the day (Garg et al. 2021; Kelly 2013);
   camps themselves move every few weeks (Hadza: about six, record only).
4. **Longer separations** occur (hunting trips, visits, travel) and fall more on men; no rate was read.
5. **Births.** Separation of spouses lowers fertility, in proportion to its length and completeness (Potter et al. 1983;
   Nie 2020; Menken 1979 record only).
6. **Nature or culture.** **Pair bonds and returning to a home base** are species-typical (Marlowe 2007 places the
   division of labour after pair bonds; central-place foraging is "a key feature" of forager organisation). The
   **division of labour** itself and **residence rules** (who moves at marriage) are partly cultural, and vary.

## What this means for ACL (read against C1)

| | real foragers | ACL |
|---|---|---|
| where a couple sleeps | the same camp, nearly every night | no camp; each partner's weekly location is where they foraged |
| daily foraging | often apart, returning to camp | partners choose tiles independently, with no return |
| a week | spouses together in camp most nights of it | apart (beyond conception distance) about half the weeks |
| what holds a couple | a shared central place, pair bond | a pull toward a partner only beyond 3 tiles, outbid by food |

At ACL's weekly step, a real forager couple would count as **together almost every week**, even when they forage apart
by day, because they share a camp. ACL's "apart about half the weeks" has no counterpart in this evidence. The gap is
**the absence of a shared central place**, not a lack of affection.

## Assistant's view (the creator decides)

L2 is the candidate the evidence anchors best: real couples are together at the scale of a week because they share a
central place, not because they forage side by side. ACL already has a personal "home" (a person's best-remembered
place), which partners do not share. Before any design, **rule 12's feasibility check, read-only:** in ACL's present
worlds, how far apart are partners' homes, and how often is each partner at or near their own home? If partners' homes
already lie close together, L1 (aligning the thresholds) may do most of the work at least cost; if not, L2 is the
anchored change. Either way the value (how strongly a home pulls) must be fixed before any run, never raised until births
rise (rule 17).
