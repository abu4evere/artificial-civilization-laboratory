## The four answers

### 1. Why trust is disconnected from truth

- **Trust is mostly about being fed.** Receiving food causes 78% of all trust movement. Next come witnesses
  hearing a wrong version of what they saw (9%) and harm (6%). Claims being tested cause 3%.
- **Tests are rare and touch one relationship.** A whole world tests about 5 claims a year. Each test moves only
  the tester's trust in whoever told them, never anyone else's.
- **Being right is luck, not a trait.** Claims hold 54% of the time, close to a coin toss. For the 887 people
  whose claims were tested 4 or more times, how often they were right early on does not predict how often they
  were right later (r = 0.03).
- **Why being right is luck:**
  - What people pass on is mostly "leave X somewhere in season S". Those beliefs come from noticing that a spot
    stood out, which is noisy and depends on the place.
  - The claim carries no place, so the listener tests it somewhere else.
  - People also pass on what they were only told, as if they knew it.
  - One outcome decides a test.
- **Conclusion:** there is no reliable truth signal for trust to follow, and what little there is gets drowned out
  by gifts. Law 92's chain (accurate predictions → trust → influence) cannot start under the current laws. Its
  first link, a reliable difference between people who are right and people who are not, never forms.

### 2. Why sowing belief spreads but sowing behaviour does not

- **The belief barely spreads either, and both fade together.**
  - Sowing peaked early (about 1,200 sowings in years 0–24) and fell to around 50 per 25 years after year 75.
  - Tested believers (belief 0.15 or more in the current season) average only 0.4–3 people at a time, against
    0.3–1.4 sowers a year, a ratio of 1–4 to 1.
  - The E1 "practice" measure (belief 0.1 or more in any season) made it look larger early on.
  - Beliefs fade slowly (half-life about 8 years). So they outlive the behaviour, but they do not grow.
- **Sowing never pays the sower.** Every unit of seed eaten is 1 food. What sowers got back per unit of seed:

  | sown in | came back to the sower | came back to anyone | best the physics allows (untouched, at peak) |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | spring | 0.37 | 1.47 | 2.77 |
  | summer | 0.41 | 1.17 | 1.55 |
  | autumn | 0.17 | 0.80 | 1.73 |
  | winter | 0.27 | 1.05 | 2.57 |

- **Why it never pays:**
  - Seeds are plentiful in autumn, so 55% of all seed is sown then.
  - Autumn seed sits in the ground until spring, losing 3% a week to animals.
  - The crop peaks the next summer, by which time the sower is usually elsewhere and others eat it. Only 10–35%
    of harvested crop went to someone who had sowed that spot.
  - Habits learn from what the sower gains, so sowing is not reinforced.
- **Believers are hungry.** In 55% of the weeks a believer could have sowed, they had less than two weeks of food,
  and 44% of the time they went for food instead. Hunger directly dampens the wish to set seed or food down, and
  seed is also food. Sowing reached their top four choices in 11% of those weeks; they sowed in 4%.
- **Why the belief survives anyway:** a belief about leaving seeds is credited when the spot later stands out
  from the land around it. That happens whether or not the believer gets any of the food.
- **Conclusion:** the belief survives because it rewards the believer for noticing. The behaviour dies because it
  doesn't reward the sower for doing.

### 3. Do islands truly isolate populations, once the crossing gap is accounted for?

- **The gap.** A person moves up to 3 tiles a week, and only the landing tile is checked. In every seed some
  islands are only 2–3 tiles apart.
- **What the gap did.** Of 5,682 crossings between islands, 5,252 (92%) were made with nothing that floats, every
  one across water 3 tiles wide or less. 1,278 of those were children carried by a carer.
- **Without the gap:** only **3 crossings in 1,462 world-years** went over water wider than a jump: one in seed 102
  (year 16) and two in seed 108 (from year 22). Even counting every crossing made while carrying something that
  floats (430 in all), most were over the same narrow channels.
- **Rafts exist but are not used.**
  - At years 100 and 200, 10–44 people per world know how to make something that floats, but only 0–3 carry one.
  - Its worth to them is about 0.1, because nothing ever judges a thing by how well it floats, and people carry
    only their three most valued things.
  - The only way a raft gets used is the 25% chance, when someone holding one explores, of heading for a random
    far shore. That target changes every week, so open water wider than one week's move is almost never crossed.
- **The evidence in ancestry.** Where islands are 2 tiles apart, by year 200 nearly everyone descends from more
  than one founding band (100% in seeds 103, 106 and 108). In seed 102, whose islands are 3 or more apart, nobody
  ever does (0% at year 100 and 200).
- **Conclusion:** with the jump gap removed, the islands would isolate their bands almost completely. Not because
  rafts are hard to make (they appear within a few years), but because a raft is never valued for floating and
  sea travel has no direction. This estimate comes from crossings recorded under the current physics. A world
  without the gap was not run, because that would be a change.

### 4. Are early inventions too easy because of the property thresholds?

- **Two kinds of threshold.**
  - The historian's naming thresholds only decide what we call a thing and when "the first …" is written. They
    change nothing in anyone's life.
  - The one physics threshold is crossing water (float 0.65 or more). Everything else works on a sliding scale.
- **What makes early inventions easy is how properties combine, not the thresholds.**
  - Tying two buoyant things adds half the smaller one's float, so two sticks make 0.75.
  - Tying flexible things adds 0.1 containment per flexible part plus layering, so three fibers already hold 0.71,
    multiplying what a person can carry by 1.7.
  - Tying stone to wood gives something hard and long.
  - 14 of the 54 possible one-step combinations of raw materials already make a named kind. In every seed the
    carrier appears in weeks 2–35, the raft in weeks 10–165, and the club within about 4 years.
- **Where it is hard, the limit is materials and depth, not thresholds.**
  - Wraps need hide, which comes only from hunting: years 5–48.
  - Spears need a blade made first: years 0–19.
- **On paper:** if crossing water needed float 0.8, no one-step tie would cross; a raft would need a second step.
  The naming thresholds could move freely without changing anything that happens.
- **Conclusion:**
  - Yes, the first useful things are one random fiddle away. That comes from the combination rules (bonuses that
    stack in one step) and the small number of possible first steps (54), weighed against how often curious people
    fiddle (every week).
  - Law 116's problem-driven path never has to work, because the opportunity path delivers immediately.
  - Raising the naming thresholds would only change our labels. Making invention less trivial would mean changing
    how properties combine, or what counts as a step. That decision is yours.

### C. Why do rafts appear immediately?

**The physics, checked without running anything.** There are 54 things a person can do in one step with raw materials (12 strikes, 42 ties). 14 of them already make a named kind: a carrier ×6, a club ×4, a blade ×2, a raft ×2, a wrap ×2.

A raft is two pieces of wood tied together. Raw wood floats 0.5. Tying two buoyant things gives `max + 0.5 × min` = 0.75, and 0.65 is enough to carry a person over water. So the very first time anyone ties two sticks, they have something that can cross the sea. Wood is the commonest material near camps, fiber the next, and an untried material pulls curious people to pick it up.

#### Early inventions and the property thresholds

Two kinds of threshold are involved, and only one changes what people can do:

- **Physics (changes lives):** something floating 0.65 or more lets a person stand on water (`world.passable`). Tools help in proportion to their properties (`matter.factor`), with no threshold.
- **Names (changes nothing):** the historian calls a thing a raft at float 0.65, a carrier at contain 0.6, a wrap at warm 0.6, a club at hard 0.6 with reach 0.55, a blade at edge 0.55. These only decide what we call things and when "the first …" is written. People never see them.
