## Sources

### 1. Boyd, R. *Cumulative Culture.* Becoming Human, Institute of Human Origins, Arizona State University (web page). Full page.
- **Claim:** only humans accumulate a wide range of adaptive knowledge across generations; complex tools (Central Inuit
  kit, Hadza bows) could not be invented by one person.
- **Evidence / method:** educational overview; no studies cited by name.
- **Limits:** secondary; no data.
- **ACL relevance (hypothesis):** frames CA1/CA2's question; gives no anchor by itself.

### 2. Caldwell, C. A., Renner, E. and Atkinson, M. (2017/18). Human teaching and cumulative cultural evolution. *Review of Philosophy and Psychology.* Full text (PMC6290649).
- **Claim (B):** teaching helps most for complex, opaque skills.
- **Evidence / method:** experiment, 155 undergraduates; knots, two simple and two complex. Learners saw the end state
  only, or step-by-step pictures, or were taught by a trained partner. Teaching raised success on complex knots
  (means about 4.6–5.0 against 2.7–3.3 for end state only); simple knots were at ceiling in every condition
  (complexity × teaching interaction, p < .001).
- **Limits:** one generation, not cumulative; teacher–learner interaction not recorded, so which part of teaching
  helped is unknown; modern adults.
- **ACL relevance (hypothesis):** in ACL a lesson passes a recipe whole and in one step; the evidence is that complex
  techniques need richer interaction than seeing the result. Nothing here says the learner must already know the parts.

### 3. Lucas, A. J., Kings, M., Whittle, D., Davey, E., Happé, F., Caldwell, C. A. and Thornton, A. (2020). The value of teaching increases with tool complexity in cumulative cultural evolution. *Proc. R. Soc. B* 287: 20201885. Abstract/record.
- **Claim (B):** in transmission chains (624 participants), both simple and complex tools improved over generations;
  teaching benefited only the complex ones; complex tools kept more design diversity.
- **Limits:** abstract only; laboratory tasks.
- **ACL relevance (hypothesis):** cumulative improvement of simple things does not need teaching; of complex things, it
  does. Consistent with CA2's G7 (composites almost never followed) as the place a richer lesson would matter.

### 4. Morgan, T. J. H., Uomini, N. T., Rendell, L. E. et al. (2015). Experimental evidence for the co-evolution of hominin tool-making teaching and language. *Nature Communications* 6: 6029. Full text (PMC4338549).
- **Claim (B, D):** stone-knapping skill transmits better with teaching and language than with imitation/emulation
  or reverse engineering.
- **Evidence / method:** transmission chains, 184 adults; five conditions from seeing flakes only to verbal teaching;
  five-minute learning. Verbal teaching nearly doubled overall flake quality against reverse engineering; viable flakes
  per strike doubled (gestural) and quadrupled (verbal). With teaching, performance **declined steadily along chains**;
  with imitation or reverse engineering it fell to a floor at once.
- **Limits:** the authors call five minutes "clearly unrealistically short"; modern adults; archaeological meaning
  (Oldowan stasis) is inference.
- **ACL relevance (hypothesis):** even good teaching loses fidelity along chains in short lessons; low-fidelity copying
  (ACL's watching) collapses at once, as E7.7 and CA1 found (copying by watching under 1% of hand-overs).

### 5. Nakata, Y. and Takezawa, M. (2023). Conditions under which faithful cultural transmission through teaching promotes cumulative cultural evolution. *Scientific Reports.* Full text (PMC10684533).
- **Claim (B, C):** teaching promotes cumulative culture only when tasks are too hard to learn alone in the time
  available; when time is ample, time spent teaching costs innovation.
- **Evidence / method:** model (reinforcement learners on multi-goal networks over generations).
- **Limits (theirs):** single learner; finite goals, not open-ended; one task per agent.
- **ACL relevance (hypothesis):** echoes FX1/E8.2: where the world does not make skills hard and valuable, teaching
  has little to carry. A world-side condition, not a teaching rule.

### 6. Lew-Levy, S., Reckin, R., Lavi, N., Cristóbal-Azkarate, J. and Ellis-Davies, K. (2017). How do hunter-gatherer children learn subsistence skills? A meta-ethnographic review. *Human Nature* 28: 367–394. Full text (PMC5662667). Living societies.
- **Claim (A, C):** learning is sequenced and mostly self-driven: infants are taken foraging and given toy tools; in
  childhood, multi-age playgroups, observation and participation; by late childhood children are proficient
  collectors; **direct instruction is largely reserved for adolescents and for complex skills such as hunting and
  multicomponent tool-making.**
- **Evidence:** 58 publications. Toy or scale-model tools across many societies (Gidra, Batek, Kaytetye, Chabu, Aka,
  Hadza, San); Gidra parents give well-made scale models to be reverse-engineered; San children make bows and arrows
  at four to five; older children correct younger ones' tool-making (San, Kaytetye); children join parts of adult work
  (Aka: "any adult activity"; Chabu: the kill). Some societies (San, Batek, Nayaka, Matsigenka) deliberately refrain
  from instructing. Mastery of big-game hunting takes decades (Ache: over 35 years).
- **Limits (theirs):** African bias; hunting over-represented; 77 years of incomparable methods; few foragers' own
  accounts; schooling confounds.
- **ACL relevance (hypothesis), question A:** real learners meet the **parts** of a complex technique before the
  whole (toy versions, sub-tasks, participation), over years. ACL's composite lesson requires the learner already to
  know every made part, and nothing in ACL sequences parts. That is a candidate explanation for G7, not a finding.

### 7. Hewlett, B. S. and Roulette, C. J. (2016). Teaching in hunter–gatherer infancy. *Royal Society Open Science* 3: 150403. Abstract/record. Living society (Aka).
- **Claim (C):** video of Aka infants shows several forms of teaching, including natural pedagogy, against the view
  that teaching is rare in small-scale societies.
- **Limits:** abstract only; infancy, not crafts.
- **ACL relevance (hypothesis):** teaching is everyday and early, inside close relationships; ACL's teaching is tied
  to talks (CA2's G1 found nearly half of lost inventions never met one).

### 8. Kline, M. A. (2015). How to learn about teaching: an evolutionary framework for the study of teaching behavior in humans and other animals. *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 38: e31. Abstract/record.
- **Claim (B, C):** teaching takes several forms: teaching by tolerance of observation, by opportunity provisioning
  (sometimes with scaffolding), by stimulus enhancement, by evaluative feedback, and by direct active teaching; each
  narrows what the learner must infer.
- **Limits:** framework, not data.
- **ACL relevance (hypothesis):** ACL has one form, a told recipe in a talk. "Opportunity provisioning" (giving a
  learner the parts or a simpler version to work on) has no counterpart in ACL.

### 9. Fogarty, L., Strimling, P. and Laland, K. N. (2011). The evolution of teaching. *Evolution* 65: 2760–2770. Abstract/record.
- **Claim (C):** in models of a tutor teaching a related pupil at a cost, teaching is favoured for information that is
  valuable and hard to acquire otherwise; cumulative culture creates such information.
- **Limits:** abstract only; model.
- **ACL relevance (hypothesis):** teaching and cumulative culture feed each other; neither starts without the other
  being partly there.

### 10. Tehrani, J. and Riede, F. (2008). Towards an archaeology of pedagogy: learning, teaching and the generation of material culture traditions. *World Archaeology* 40: 316–331. Abstract/record. Archaeological inference plus ethnography.
- **Claim (A, D, E):** craft apprenticeship scaffolds skill gradually through **demonstration, intervention and
  collaboration**, which produces faithful, lasting traditions (a *chaîne opératoire* learned in stages).
- **Limits:** abstract only; how prehistoric people learned is inferred.
- **ACL relevance (hypothesis):** collaboration (working on a shared piece, the novice doing part of the sequence) is
  absent from ACL.

### 11. Stout, D. (2002). Skill and cognition in stone tool production: an ethnographic case study from Irian Jaya. *Current Anthropology* 43(5). Abstract/record. Living tradition (Langda adze makers).
- **Claim (A, C):** adze-making is learned in an apprenticeship of five years or more, with the community of makers as
  a social scaffold; skill combines perceptual-motor ability with a large body of technical knowledge.
- **Limits:** abstract only (full text not retrieved); one community; specialist craft in a farming society, not
  foragers.
- **ACL relevance (hypothesis):** complex crafts take years of supported practice; ACL skill grows only by doing alone.

### 12. Legare, C. H. (2019). The development of cumulative cultural learning. *Annual Review of Developmental Psychology* 1: 119–147. Abstract/record.
- **Claim (A, B):** children combine exploration, observation, imitation (including faithful copying of steps whose
  purpose is unclear), participation and instruction; how much each is used varies by culture.
- **Limits:** abstract only.
- **ACL relevance (hypothesis):** faithful imitation of a whole sequence, without understanding, is a human route to
  learning complex techniques; ACL has no route to copying a sequence of steps.

## Across the sources, by question (notes, not conclusions)

- **A. Prerequisites.** In every living-society source, learners meet parts or simpler versions first (toy tools,
  sub-tasks, participation, apprenticeship stages) and the whole later, over years. No source describes a rule that
  a learner must already know every part before being shown a whole. (Sources 6, 10, 11.)
- **B. Teaching versus showing.** Teaching beats seeing the product mainly for complex skills (2, 3, 4); for simple
  ones, seeing is enough. Direct instruction for complex crafts comes late (adolescence) (6).
- **C. Costs and who is taught.** Teaching happens within close, repeated relationships and is selective; some foraging
  peoples avoid instructing at all (6, 7). Models favour teaching only where skills are both valuable and hard (5, 9).
- **D. Fidelity.** Short lessons decay along chains even with teaching; copying the product alone collapses (4).
- **E.** Archaeological sources (4's interpretation, 10) infer learning from sequences of manufacture; the living
  sources (6, 7, 11) observe it.

## Two hypotheses for the Law 126 proposal GPT asked for (to be weighed only after CA3; nothing adopted)

- **Opportunity:** knowledge fails to spread because inventors rarely meet suitable learners in time (CA2's G1).
- **Prerequisite:** knowledge fails to spread because learners cannot reproduce the parts of a composite (CA2's G7);
  the living evidence suggests real learners meet parts first, which ACL has no way to do.

Both may be true. GPT's warning stands: "The danger is building a teaching system that secretly manufactures
cumulative culture."
