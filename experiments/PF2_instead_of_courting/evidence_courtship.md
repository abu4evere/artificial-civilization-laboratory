# How real foragers form couples, and how much time courting competes with food (evidence notes, 21 October 2026)

Why: PF1 and PF2 found that in ACL, a suitable partner is usually within reach but courting almost never wins against
getting food (about one chance in a thousand), and a stranger can almost never accept. Before any change to courting
(a Law 126 change to nature), this note collects what is known about real foragers. **Notes only; nothing is
proposed.** Each source says how much of it was read: **full text**, **abstract/record**, or **secondary** (known
only through another source or a search summary).

## Sources

### 1. Walker, R. S., Hill, K. R., Flinn, M. V. and Ellsworth, R. M. (2011). Evolutionary history of hunter-gatherer marriage practices. *PLoS ONE* 6(4): e19066. **Full text (article page).**
- Citing Apostolou's comparative sample: "arrangement of marriage by parents or close kin is the primary mode of
  marriage in **85%** of the sample"; in their own phylogenetic sample of 15 societies, **67%** arranged against
  courtship marriages.
- "Brideservice, brideprice, or some type of exchange between families is found in **80%** of the sample."
- Arranged marriage is inferred to go back at least to the first migrations out of Africa; earlier is equivocal
  because several African foragers have courtship marriages.

### 2. Apostolou, M. (2007). Sexual selection under parental choice: the role of parents in the evolution of human mating. *Evolution and Human Behavior* (November 2007). **Abstract/record.**
- 190 hunting and gathering societies: almost all reproduction happens within marriage, and marriage is regulated by
  parents and close kin; daughters' choices are more controlled than sons'.

### 3. Marlowe, F. W. (Hadza; including *The Hadza*, 2010, already in the book's sources). **Secondary** (search summaries of his work; the book itself not re-read for this note).
- Among the Hadza, marriages are **not arranged**: first marriages follow brief courtship; female choice seems the main
  factor; serial monogamy with fairly frequent divorce. The Hadza are one of the African exceptions Walker et al.
  mention.

### 4. Sayre, M. K., Anyawire, M., Paolo, B., Mabulla, A. Z. P., Pontzer, H., Wood, B. M. and Raichlen, D. A. (2023). Lifestyle and patterns of physical activity in Hadza foragers. *American Journal of Biological Anthropology* 182(3): 340–356. **Abstract/record** (the full text was behind a verification page).
- Hadza adults spend a good deal of time on non-subsistence activities such as visiting friends and family; inactive
  time about 11–12 hours a day (as reported in the search summary of the article).

### 5. Lee, R. B. (Ju/'hoansi, Dobe; 1968 onward). **Secondary.**
- The often-cited figure: about 12–19 hours a week getting food, the rest of the time largely social. (Later work
  argues these figures leave out processing and other work; treat as an upper bound on free time.)

## What this suggests for ACL (hypotheses, not findings)

- **Pair formation in most foragers is not a weekly individual choice weighed against foraging.** In most societies
  it runs through families: parents or kin arrange or strongly shape the match, often with exchange between families.
  Where courtship is free (the Hadza), it is brief and leads quickly to living together. In ACL, courting is an
  individual option scored each week against food, and acceptance needs prior affection or respect, so strangers
  almost never pair.
- **Real foragers have time for social life.** Subsistence takes a few hours a day; much of the rest is social. In
  ACL, food wins about three choices in four in the weeks a partner is near, and courting almost never wins.
- **A caution about ACL's principles.** Arranged marriage, bride service and exchange between families are **culture**:
  under "nature, not history" they must not be written into ACL. What could be nature (each needing a Law 126 case):
  how strongly an unpartnered adult is drawn to court at all; how a stranger's attraction is judged; how much
  attention the nearest 12 people get; and how food and social time trade off in the choice.
- A next step, if the creator wants one: anchor those candidate changes to measured forager behaviour (for example,
  how many potential partners a forager meets in a year and how quickly couples form after first meeting), before any
  study is drafted.
