Bro, my main view is that both notes are pointing at real gaps between ACL and human life, but we should measure the missing mechanisms before changing nature. Here are my recommendations for your evidence notes.

Note 1 — How real foragers form couples
Q1. Is your reading sound 

Mostly, but I would narrow the conclusion.

Your sources support the idea that pair formation in many forager societies involves more than two isolated people independently deciding whether to court. Kin, social relationships, repeated contact, and cultural marriage practices can all matter.

Apostolou (2007) reports that parental or close-kin control played a role in marriage across most of his 190-society sample. 
ScienceDirect
Walker et al. (2011) report arranged marriage as the primary mode in 85% of Apostolou's full sample and 67% of their smaller 15-society phylogenetic sample. They also find widespread family exchange practices. But they explicitly acknowledge uncertainty about the earliest ancestral marriage pattern. 
PLOS
The Hadza are an important counterexample: Marlowe's work describes marriage as generally not arranged, female choice as important, and serial monogamy as characteristic. 
PubMed Central (PMC)

My objection is to jumping from marriage is socially organized to pair formation is primarily a family process. Marriage customs and the process by which attraction, courtship, and co-residence develop are related but different things.

Likewise, the finding that foragers spend relatively limited time obtaining subsistence does not automatically mean courtship competes weakly with food. Time available is not the same as motivation, opportunity, or probability of pairing.

What I would measure

I'd prioritize these in order:

Potential-partner exposure: How often does an unpartnered adult encounter eligible, unrelated adults of the relevant age range  Measure encounters per person-year, not simply population size.
Relationship progression: Of observed potential-partner encounters, how many lead to courtship, mutual interest, co-residence, and a lasting partnership  Record time from first known contact to each stage.
Prior familiarity: Are eventual partners already known to each other  Measure relationship duration and prior interaction before courtship.
Social mediation: How often do relatives or other community members introduce, encourage, oppose, or negotiate potential partnerships  Keep these behaviors separate from formal marriage arrangements.
Competing activities: When courtship is possible but does not occur, what does the person actually do instead—subsistence, childcare, rest, socializing, or something else 
Acceptance and rejection: What predicts mutual interest and successful pairing  Look at prior familiarity, reciprocity, age, kinship restrictions, and observed preferences rather than assuming attraction is just one universal score.

The most important comparison with PF1/PF2 is not just “how much courting happens ” It is “what opportunities existed, what happened next, and what alternative action was chosen ”

ACL's roughly 1-in-1,000 median courtship choice share is a property of its current decision system. It should not be treated as an estimate of human courtship motivation.

My nature-versus-culture objection

Your distinction is useful, but “nature” does not mean “biologically innate,” and “culture” does not mean “irrelevant to a naturalistic simulation.”

A nature-level rule could allow agents to:

become familiar through repeated contact;
develop preferences through interaction;
reciprocate or reject interest;
spend time socializing without an immediate reproductive outcome;
form social ties that affect later decisions.

Those are possible general mechanisms, not claims that every forager society uses them identically.

By contrast, automatically arranging marriages through parents, instituting bride service, or prescribing a particular marriage custom would encode specific social institutions. I agree those should not be added merely because they appear in ethnographic records.

My recommendation: Before changing ACL, run a read-only audit of the current courtship decision process. Log eligible encounters, familiarity, action chosen instead of courtship, attempts, acceptance, and eventual pairing. This will tell you which mechanism is actually producing the bottleneck.

Note 2 — How real foragers learn tool use
Q2. Is your preregistered expectation sound 

Yes, with one important qualification.

Your expectation is reasonable:

If TU1 finds mostly individual discovery and little spread, that would be consistent with ACL missing important opportunities for social learning, including access to tools, play with tool-like objects, and learning through observation or participation.

But the result would not prove that missing object-transfer or toy routes caused the low spread. Low spread could also arise from the current incentives to learn, memory limits, poor opportunities to observe skilled agents, the complexity of the tool, or the cost of using it.

And if tools spread poorly, that does not establish that the tools are useless. Usefulness, discovery, learning, retention, and transmission are separate outcomes.

What your sources tell us
Lew-Levy et al. (2017): Their review of 58 publications describes learning beginning in infancy, including toy tools and participation in foraging; play, observation, and participation are important during childhood, while direct teaching of complex skills becomes more prominent in adolescence. 
PubMed Central (PMC)
Lew-Levy et al. (2022): Their dataset of 434 observations from 54 hunter-gatherer societies finds that children's objects are often used in play and are frequently full-sized or miniature versions of adult tools. This supports investigating play and access to tools—not assuming every society uses the same toy-learning system. 
Frontiers
Garfield, Garfield   Hewlett (2016): Their cross-cultural analysis of 23 hunter-gatherer populations codes teaching as 58% of cultural-transmission cases and observation/imitation as 22%. These are proportions of coded cases, not percentages of all real-world learning events. 
Human Relations Area Files
Hewlett   Roulette (2016): Their study of Aka hunter-gatherers provides evidence relevant to teaching in infancy, making it useful for thinking about how learning can begin before formal instruction. 
PubMed Central (PMC)
Which missing routes could be nature-level 

I'd distinguish the general learning mechanism from the particular cultural behavior.

Possible route	Nature-level candidate 	Reason
Observing someone use a tool	Yes	Agents can learn from visible behavior without an explicit lesson.
Trying to imitate a demonstrated action	Yes	A general learning mechanism, though its effectiveness needs evidence.
Children playing with miniature or simplified tools	Yes, as a general capacity	Play and exploration can be allowed without scripting specific toys or traditions.
Access to tools made or held by others	Yes, potentially	Object access and possession are mechanics; particular sharing customs are cultural.
Teaching or demonstrating a skill	Potentially	A general behavior can emerge from social interaction and learning incentives.
Inheriting tools	Already part of ACL, per your description	Audit how often inheritance gives access and whether recipients use or learn from the object.
Trade, gift-giving rules, apprenticeship customs	Usually culture-specific	These can be investigated later, rather than hard-coded from one society.

One crucial distinction: holding a tool is not knowing how to use it. ACL should track at least possession/access, observed use, successful use, knowledge of the method, and whether that knowledge spreads to another agent.

What I'd measure in TU1's saved worlds
How many tool users discovered the tool independently 
How many agents first used it after observing someone else 
How many possessed or accessed a tool but never used it 
How often did children encounter adult tools or tool users 
How often did an agent have an opportunity to observe a useful tool action but fail to learn it 
Did tool use spread beyond the inventor's immediate social circle 
Once learned, how long did use persist, and did it improve the agent's outcomes 

Keep the units and denominators explicit. For example, “percentage of tool users who learned socially” is different from “percentage of observation opportunities that resulted in learning.”

My bottom line for the evidence notes

Note 1: Your interpretation is plausible but too broad if stated as “forager pair formation runs through families.” The evidence supports substantial cultural variation and, in many societies, kin influence. First investigate ACL's actual opportunity-to-action pathway and distinguish courtship from marriage.

Note 2: Your preregistered expectation is sound as a diagnostic interpretation, provided you say “consistent with missing learning routes,” not “proves the missing routes caused the result.” Investigate observation, play, access, and transmission separately before adding mechanics.

The principle I would protect across both notes: Human evidence should help identify mechanisms worth testing, not tell ACL which history to reproduce.
