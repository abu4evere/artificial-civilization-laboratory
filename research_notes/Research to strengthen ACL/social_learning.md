# Social learning in humans (foragers and children): when watching becomes lasting skill

Scope: evidence notes for the Artificial Civilization Laboratory (ACL). ACL's current design is that agents learn by watching in only two narrow situations; successful "planners" are seen and respected but not copied; what spreads is a habit, not a plan; useful techniques can sit in plain sight for a lifetime without being learned; and skills degrade as they pass down generations. These notes check each of those assumptions against the literature.

How sources were checked (October 2026). Every DOI below was resolved this session through Europe PMC (which returns the abstract) or Crossref (metadata only). Each source carries a label:
- **full text read**: the full article was fetched. Note that extraction went through an automated summariser, so quoted numbers are as it reported them.
- **abstract read**: the Europe PMC abstract was retrieved.
- **record only**: only the bibliographic record was confirmed. Any statement about its content comes from my prior knowledge and is flagged as such.

## 1. Social learning strategies and biases: when to copy, and whom

### Takeaway
Theory and experiments agree that copying pays mainly because demonstrators filter information: they show behaviours that have already paid off. People copy more when individual learning is costly or uncertain. They choose models by domain-specific success, and use prestige as a proxy when success itself cannot be seen. The 2018 and later literature stresses that people switch flexibly between strategies rather than following one fixed rule. Prestige-bias evidence is real but mixed across tasks.

### Cited Findings
- **Rendell et al. 2010.** In a computer tournament where social and asocial learning cost the same, strategies that relied heavily on social learning did very well. The reason given is that individuals inadvertently filter information for copiers by performing their highest-payoff behaviours. The winner, "discountmachine", learned almost entirely socially and weighted information by how recently it was acquired. *Abstract read.* — [Rendell, Boyd, Cownden, Enquist, Eriksson, Feldman, Fogarty, Ghirlanda, Lillicrap & Laland 2010, Science 328(5975):208–213, doi:10.1126/science.1184719](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1184719)
- **Laland 2004.** Proposed the taxonomy of "when" rules (copy when asocial learning is costly; copy when uncertain) and "who" rules (copy the successful; copy the majority; copy kin). The record is verified; the content summary is from my prior knowledge and was not re-read. *Record only.* — [Laland 2004, Learning & Behavior 32:4–14, doi:10.3758/BF03196002](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03196002)
- **Kendal et al. 2018.** Updated the social learning strategy concept: individuals switch between strategies flexibly and use several at once, depending on context. The authors reject fixed one-to-one links between psychological mechanisms and population-level patterns. *Abstract read.* — [Kendal, Boogert, Rendell, Laland, Webster & Jones 2018, Trends Cogn Sci 22(7):651–665, doi:10.1016/j.tics.2018.04.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2018.04.003)
- **Morgan et al. 2012.** Experimental test of when humans copy. Crossref dates the record 2011 (online first); the print volume is 279 (2012). Content not re-read; my prior knowledge is that copying increased with task difficulty and with lower confidence. *Record only.* — [Morgan, Rendell, Ehn, Hoppitt & Laland, Proc R Soc B 279:653–662, doi:10.1098/rspb.2011.1172](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2011.1172)
- **Henrich & Gil-White 2001.** Prestige is "freely conferred deference". Learners ingratiate themselves with successful models to gain proximity and long interaction, and newcomers can read who is being deferred to as a cue for whom to learn from. Prestige is distinct from force-based dominance. *Abstract read.* — [Henrich & Gil-White 2001, Evol Hum Behav 22(3):165–196, doi:10.1016/S1090-5138(00)00071-4](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1090-5138(00)00071-4)
- **Chudek et al. 2012.** The odds of 3–4-year-olds learning from an adult whom bystanders had attended to were more than twice the odds of learning from an ignored adult. The number comes from a search snippet; *record verified, abstract not read directly.* — [Chudek, Heller, Birch & Henrich 2012, Evol Hum Behav 33:46–56, doi:10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2011.05.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2011.05.005)
- **Jiménez & Mesoudi 2019.** Review of prestige-biased learning, which notes mixed evidence. In one example, a larger follow-up study found children no more likely to copy irrelevant actions from a high-prestige demonstrator than from a low-prestige one. *Record only, plus search snippet.* — [Jiménez & Mesoudi 2019, Palgrave Communications 5:20, doi:10.1057/s41599-019-0228-7](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-019-0228-7)
- **Brand, Heap, Morgan & Mesoudi 2020.** Online experiment (N=269) where prestige was measured as how often someone was copied. Participants used prestige when it was an indirect cue of success and when direct success information was unavailable. *Abstract read.* — [Sci Rep 10:12095, doi:10.1038/s41598-020-68982-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-68982-4)
- **Brand, Mesoudi & Morgan 2021.** Online quiz across four domains (397 participants started). People strongly preferred domain-specific prestige over domain-general prestige, over cross-domain prestige, over random cues. They kept this preference even though domain-general cues carried four times more information. This matches the data: same-topic scores correlated across rounds at r=0.47–0.78, versus r=0.24–0.60 across topics. *Full text read.* — [PLOS ONE 16(8):e0255346, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0255346](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0255346)
- **Deffner, Kleinow & McElreath 2020.** Preregistered experiment (N=237, mostly German) that varied the environment over space and time. After spatial change (simulated migration), people leaned more on conformist learning than after change over time. After both kinds of change, people were biased toward experienced group members. Social learning rates fell quickly after migration, while conformity persisted. *Abstract read.* — [R Soc Open Sci 7(12):200734, doi:10.1098/rsos.200734](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.200734)
- **Wood, Kendal & Flynn 2013.** "Whom do children copy?" is a review of model-based biases in children: age, competence, familiarity, and similar cues. Content not re-read. *Record only.* — [Wood, Kendal & Flynn 2013, Developmental Review 33:341–356, doi:10.1016/j.dr.2013.08.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dr.2013.08.002)

### Inferences (mapping to ACL)
- **"Planners are respected but not copied" runs against the main evidence.** In theory (Henrich & Gil-White) and in experiments (Chudek; Brand 2020, 2021), prestige mostly works by steering attention and copying toward a model. Two findings do support part of ACL's rule:
  - (a) Prestige transfers poorly across domains (Brand 2021). Respect earned for planning need not cause copying of, for example, toolmaking.
  - (b) People copy what they can observe. A "plan" is internal and opaque. What an observer can copy is the visible behaviour and its outcome, so "copy the habit, not the plan" is defensible.
- **Suggestion (evidence-anchored, does not hand agents the answer).** Let respect or prestige raise the *probability and duration of attending* to a model in that model's domain, rather than transferring the plan itself. What gets transmitted is the observed action and its context (where, with what object). The learner must still reproduce it through its own practice and get the payoff before the habit sticks.
- **Gate "when to copy" on the agent's own uncertainty and its recent failures in that domain** (Laland 2004; Morgan 2012; Kendal 2018). After a group moves to a new area, raise reliance on experienced residents (Deffner 2020). Neither rule tells the agent what to do.

### Gaps
- I found no forager field study that directly tests prestige bias versus success bias in choosing subsistence models. Lew-Levy et al. report that BaYaka adolescents nominate "accessible" adult men as spear-hunting models, but I could not verify that record this session.
- I did not retrieve 2023–2026 critiques of the social learning strategies framework beyond Kendal 2018 and Brand 2020/2021.

## 2. How hunter-gatherer children and adolescents learn foraging and tool skills

### Takeaway
Among foragers, observation is pervasive and by far the most common learning activity. Actual skill comes from playing with miniature or real tools, participating in tasks, and trial and error inside mixed-age child playgroups. Children are competent food collectors by late middle childhood. Complex hunting and making multi-part tools are learned in adolescence, with some teaching, and hunting skill keeps improving into the 30s.

### Cited Findings
- **Hewlett et al. 2011, Aka and Bofi (Congo Basin).** *Full text read.*
  - Transmission is mainly vertical (parents) up to age 4–5. Horizontal (peers) and oblique (other adults) transmission dominate from about 5 to 12.
  - By about age 10, children know most subsistence, childcare and sharing skills.
  - Boyette's time-allocation data for 4–18-year-olds: observation took about 72 min/day; children received instruction for about 14 min/day, gave instruction for about 5 min/day, and imitated for about 3 min/day.
  - An egalitarian ethos makes adults unlikely to feel they "know what is best" for a child.
  - Source: [Hewlett, Fouts, Boyette & Hewlett 2011, Phil Trans R Soc B 366(1567):1168–1178, doi:10.1098/rstb.2010.0373](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2010.0373)
- **Lew-Levy et al. 2017, meta-ethnography of 58 publications.** *Full text read.*
  - Infants are given toy tools.
  - Early and middle childhood: multi-age playgroups where children learn through play, observation and participation. Most children are competent food collectors by late middle childhood.
  - Adolescence is when direct teaching of complex hunting and tool manufacture by adults (not only parents) begins. Learning continues across the lifetime for complex resources.
  - Several societies (San, Nayaka, Batek, Matsigenka, Yukaghir) "actively refrain from instructing, directing, explaining, or correcting". Learning is described as trial and error.
  - Batek boys choose their own hunting mentors and trail them. Chabu adolescents choose teachers by hunting and teaching ability. Adolescents seek out prestigious innovators.
  - Ache men are reported to need more than 35 years to become proficient hunters. Gidra hunting success peaks at 35–45.
  - Source: [Lew-Levy, Reckin, Lavi, Cristóbal-Azkarate & Ellis-Davies 2017, Human Nature 28(4):367–394, doi:10.1007/s12110-017-9302-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12110-017-9302-2)
- **Salali et al. 2019, BaYaka (video and time budgets).** Young children learned mostly by observation and imitation. From early childhood, learning happened mainly in playgroups and through practice. Teaching appeared for abstract knowledge such as social norms, while observation transmitted visually transparent skills such as tool use and cooking. *Abstract read.* — [Salali, Chaudhary, Bouer, Thompson, Vinicius & Migliano 2019, Sci Rep 9:11080, doi:10.1038/s41598-019-47515-8](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-019-47515-8)
- **Lew-Levy, Andersen, Lavi & Riede 2022.** 434 records of children's toys and tools from 54 hunter-gatherer societies. Most objects served both play and instrumental use. Children made their own toys. Most objects were full-size or miniature adult tools. *Abstract read.* — [Frontiers in Psychology 13:824983, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2022.824983](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.824983)
- **Lew-Levy et al. 2022.** 690 children aged 3–18 in 12 hunter-gatherer and mixed-subsistence societies. Risk from dangerous animals and dehydration predicted less childcare and domestic work, but not less food production. Gender differences were larger where men contributed more food. *Abstract read.* — [Lew-Levy, Reckin, Kissler et al. 2022, Sci Rep 12:8054, doi:10.1038/s41598-022-12217-1](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-12217-1)
- **Koster et al. 2020.** About 23,000 hunting records from more than 1,800 hunters at 40 sites. Hunting returns peak at about age 30–35, and high skill persists through much of adulthood. Within a site, variation among individuals came more from differing rates of decline than from differing rates of acquisition. *Abstract read.* — [Koster, McElreath, Hill et al. 2020, Science Advances 6(26):eaax9070, doi:10.1126/sciadv.aax9070](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aax9070)
- **Hewlett et al. 2024 (PNAS review).** About half of children's and adolescents' horizontal and oblique social learning came from individuals who were not genetic relatives. Earlier work underestimated how much vertical (family) transmission conserves culture. The authors propose "cumulative transmission" as a distinct mode. *Abstract read; full text blocked (HTTP 403).* — [Hewlett, Boyette, Lew-Levy, Gallois & Dira 2024, PNAS 121(48):e2322883121, doi:10.1073/pnas.2322883121](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2322883121)
- **Garfield, Garfield & Hewlett 2016.** Cross-cultural analysis of hunter-gatherer social learning. Record verified; content not read. *Record only.* — [Garfield, Garfield & Hewlett 2016, in *Social Learning and Innovation in Contemporary Hunter-Gatherers* (Springer), pp. 19–34, doi:10.1007/978-4-431-55997-9_2](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-4-431-55997-9_2)
- **Migliano et al. 2020, Agta.** Sensor-mapped camp networks plus simulation. Multilevel sociality (family clusters within camps, kin-based transmission, frequent moves between camps) sped up cumulative culture despite low population density. *Abstract read.* — [Migliano et al. 2020, Science Advances 6(9):eaax5913, doi:10.1126/sciadv.aax5913](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aax5913)

### Inferences (mapping to ACL)
- **ACL's "only two narrow situations" for watching is far narrower than the ethnography.** Forager children watch for more than an hour a day (Hewlett 2011). The literature does not, however, say that watching alone produces skill. The robust pattern is: watch, then try it in play with miniature or real tools, then practise in a group of peers, then refine through feedback. Adults rarely correct.
- **Suggestion.** Widen *exposure* rather than *transfer*:
  - Any agent within sight of an action can store a weak trace, for example "object X used at location Y produced food".
  - That trace only biases what the agent tries during its own exploration, especially in a play or idle state.
  - It becomes a habit only after the agent's own successful repetitions.
  - This keeps "habit, not plan" and does not give agents the answer.
- **Make learning age-structured.**
  - Under about 5: the watching pool is the parents.
  - About 5–12: mixed-age peer groups and other adults, with about half of models non-kin (Hewlett 2024).
  - Adolescents: actively choose skilled, accessible adult models for hunting.
  - Hunting proficiency should keep rising into the 30s (Koster 2020; Lew-Levy 2017). This anchors the "nature" parameters.
- **Model camp structure.** Multilevel camps with visits between camps (Migliano 2020) are a natural, unscripted way to widen who an agent can observe.

### Gaps
- I could not get per-skill acquisition curves with confidence intervals across many societies in this pass. Lew-Levy 2017 gives qualitative ages per society.
- Garfield et al. 2016 was not read, so I make no claims about its quantitative coding.

## 3. Teaching in foragers: how common, and what kinds

### Takeaway
Teaching exists among foragers but is a minority channel, about one fifth of observation time in Boyette's Aka data. It is mostly brief and non-verbal or embedded in activity: commands, negative feedback, demonstration, giving access to tools. New cross-cultural evidence shows teaching attaches more to opaque content (values, kinship, norms) than to subsistence skills, and teaching between children is common.

### Cited Findings
- **Garfield & Lew-Levy 2025, 23 egalitarian forager societies.** Teaching is more strongly associated with transmitting cultural values and kinship knowledge than subsistence skills, and is linked to opaque culture and to leadership. *Abstract read; full text redirected to a login page.* — [Garfield & Lew-Levy 2025, Nature Communications 16:3387, doi:10.1038/s41467-025-58764-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-58764-9)
- **Kline 2015.** Classifies teaching types by adaptive function. The framework says teaching narrows the range of possible inferences and so supports faithful transmission, and it treats some teaching types as uniquely human. *Abstract read.* — [Kline 2015, Behav Brain Sci 38:e31, doi:10.1017/S0140525X14000090](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X14000090)
- **Boyette & Hewlett 2017/2018.** Crossref lists vol. 9, pp. 771–797 (online 2017, print issue 2018). Content not read this session, so I make no claims from it. *Record only.* — ["Teaching in hunter-gatherers", Review of Philosophy and Psychology, doi:10.1007/s13164-017-0347-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13164-017-0347-2)
- **Lew-Levy et al. 2020, child-to-child teaching.** Focal follows of 35 Hadza and 38 BaYaka 3–18-year-olds, analysed with the Social Relations Model. Forager children spend much time in child-only groups, so many potential child teachers are available. Teaching was more likely between siblings among the Hadza. A multistage model (young children learn from peers, older children from adults) fit the BaYaka but not the Hadza, which the authors attribute to differences in subsistence and settlement. *Search snippet plus Crossref record.* — [Lew-Levy, Kissler, Boyette, Crittenden, Mabulla & Hewlett 2020, Evol Hum Behav 41:12–22, doi:10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2019.07.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2019.07.003)
- **Lew-Levy 2017 review.** The most common teaching forms are commands, negative feedback (often about sharing), and positive feedback. Demonstration with verbal instruction appears for hunting (Chabu, Penan). Adults correct tool-making errors (San, Batek, Kaytetye). Adults provide opportunities by giving access to resources. Direct instruction is "rare" and most common in adolescence. *Full text read.* — [Lew-Levy et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12110-017-9302-2)
- **Hewlett 2011.** Teaching was observed from about 12 months: moving an infant's arm to show digging or knife use, and pointing. *Full text read.* — [Hewlett et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2010.0373)
- **Morgan et al. 2015, Oldowan knapping transmission chains (N=184).** Teaching, and especially language, improved transmission; imitation and emulation did not. The authors link low-fidelity transmission to the roughly 700,000-year stasis of Oldowan technology. *Abstract read.* — [Morgan, Uomini, Rendell et al. 2015, Nat Commun 6:6029, doi:10.1038/ncomms7029](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms7029)

### Inferences (mapping to ACL)
- **ACL's choice that subsistence skills spread mostly without explicit instruction is well supported** (Garfield & Lew-Levy 2025; Hewlett 2011).
- **A minimal, evidence-anchored teaching layer could be:**
  - (a) "opportunity provision": a competent agent tolerates or allows a learner to handle its tool or join its task;
  - (b) negative feedback that interrupts a learner's failing attempt.
  
  Neither transmits a plan. Both raise how often the learner practises, and how accurately.
- **Teaching between children should be allowed**, and arguably more frequent than teaching from adults to children in mid-childhood (Lew-Levy 2020).
- **For hard-to-see (opaque), multi-step techniques such as knapping-like skills, observation alone should transmit poorly** (Morgan 2015). That supports ACL's "techniques sit unlearned in plain sight" specifically for opaque skills.

### Gaps
- I did not obtain per-hour teaching rates beyond Boyette's Aka figures, and did not read Boyette & Hewlett's taxonomy in full.
- I could not read Garfield & Lew-Levy 2025 in full, so effect sizes are unknown.

## 4. Over-imitation, copying fidelity, and what cumulative culture needs

### Takeaway
Children copy causally irrelevant actions from about 18 months, robustly and in many cultures (including San/Bushman and Aboriginal Australian children). But the size of the effect varies: Aka forager children over-imitated the least and emulated most. Models and experiments make fidelity the key bottleneck for cumulative culture. Yet technologies can improve across generations without learners understanding why they work.

### Cited Findings
- **Lyons, Young & Keil 2007.** Children automatically encode all of an adult's actions as causally meaningful, even plainly unnecessary ones. This makes over-imitation hard to extinguish, even under time pressure and after direct warnings. *Abstract read.* — [PNAS 104(50):19751–19756, doi:10.1073/pnas.0704452104](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0704452104)
- **Nielsen & Tomaselli 2010.** Over-imitation was similar in Kalahari Bushman children and children from industrialised cities, unaffected by age, testing environment, or familiarity with the demonstrator. The authors argue it is a universal human trait. *Abstract read.* — [Psychological Science 21(5):729–736, doi:10.1177/0956797610368808](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797610368808)
- **Nielsen, Mushin, Tomaselli & Whiten 2014.** 3–6-year-olds over-imitated at high rates in Western Australian, Bushman (N=64) and Aboriginal (N=19) communities, and generalised what they learned across objects. *Abstract read.* — [Child Development 85(6):2169–2184, doi:10.1111/cdev.12265](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12265)
- **Berl & Hewlett 2015.** Aka forager children aged 3–6 showed the lowest over-imitation rates recorded under comparable conditions. Among children who succeeded at the task, 60% of Aka children used emulation, compared with 15% of Ngandu farmer children, 11% of Aka adults, and 0% of Western children. *Abstract read.* — [PLOS ONE 10(3):e0120180, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0120180](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0120180)
- **Hoehl et al. 2019.** Review of a decade of over-imitation research. Content not read. *Record only.* — [Hoehl, Keupp, Schleihauf, McGuigan, Buttelmann & Whiten 2019, Developmental Review 51:90–108, doi:10.1016/j.dr.2018.12.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dr.2018.12.002)
- **Lewis & Laland 2012.** In their model, small increases in transmission fidelity are enough to produce cumulative culture ("ratcheting"). Fidelity was the primary driver, and combining traits contributed more than new invention or modification. *Abstract read.* — [Phil Trans R Soc B 367(1599):2171–2180, doi:10.1098/rstb.2012.0119](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2012.0119)
- **Derex, Bonnefon, Boyd & Mesoudi 2019.** Physical artefacts improved across generations of learners without causal understanding. Passing on causal theories had no measurable effect on the pace of improvement, and inaccurate theories constrained the next learners' exploration. *Abstract read.* — [Nature Human Behaviour 3(5):446–452, doi:10.1038/s41562-019-0567-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-019-0567-9)
- **Henrich 2004.** Demographic model of cultural loss, using Tasmania as the case. Content not re-read. From my prior knowledge: imperfect copying combined with a small pool of learners leads to skill loss. That interpretation is debated in later literature, which I did not retrieve here. *Record only.* — [Henrich 2004, American Antiquity 69:197–214, doi:10.2307/4128416](https://doi.org/10.2307/4128416)

### Inferences (mapping to ACL)
- **"Skills degrade as they pass down" is well anchored** in low-fidelity transmission (Lewis & Laland 2012; Morgan 2015; Henrich 2004).
- **But degradation should depend on the number of potential models and on fidelity**, not be a fixed per-generation decay. Specifically:
  - Bigger or better-connected pools of models offset loss (Henrich 2004, record only; Migliano 2020).
  - Children's high-fidelity, over-imitative copying could raise fidelity when they observe, though forager children may emulate more (Berl & Hewlett 2015).
- **Derex 2019 supports ACL's "habit, not plan".** Gains can be carried in copied action sequences without understanding, so agents do not need a causal model to accumulate improvements.
- **Suggestion.** Make copy fidelity a nature parameter that varies with age (high in young children), with transparency (visible end-state versus hidden manipulation), and with repeated exposures. Do not script outcomes.

### Gaps
- I found no forager field data quantifying how fast a specific skill is lost across generations. The Tasmanian case is contested, and I did not retrieve the critiques this pass.
- I did not retrieve 2020–2026 over-imitation studies in foragers beyond Berl & Hewlett 2015.

## 5. Contrast with great apes, and the "zone of latent solutions"

### Takeaway
Great apes have real traditions and can copy in diffusion experiments. Much ape culture, though, may consist of behaviours individuals could reinvent on their own (the "zone of latent solutions"), spread by enhancement effects and copying of outcomes. Human cumulative culture adds process copying, teaching and conformity.

### Cited Findings
- **Tennie, Call & Tomasello 2009.** Chimpanzee traditions are biases within behaviour the species can already produce (the zone of latent solutions), spread by founder effects and copying of products. Humans show the "ratchet" of accumulated modifications, enabled by copying processes, teaching, conformity and norms. *Abstract read.* — [Phil Trans R Soc B 364(1528):2405–2415, doi:10.1098/rstb.2009.0052](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2009.0052)
- **Whiten, McGuigan, Marshall-Pescini & Hopper 2009.** Diffusion experiments show chimpanzees copying local traditions. In "ghost" conditions, where objects move with no visible actor, chimpanzees failed, so they rely more on watching an actor than once thought. Children over-imitate unselectively. Chimpanzees showed conservatism in early cumulative-learning tests. *Abstract read.* — [Phil Trans R Soc B 364(1528):2417–2428, doi:10.1098/rstb.2009.0069](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2009.0069)
- **Tennie, Premo, Braun & McPherron 2017.** Argues that early stone tools need not imply cultural transmission, framed as "resetting the null hypothesis". Content not read. *Record only.* — [Current Anthropology 58:652–672, doi:10.1086/693846](https://doi.org/10.1086/693846)
- **Whiten 2021.** "The burgeoning reach of animal culture". Content not read. *Record only.* — [Science 372:eabe6514, doi:10.1126/science.abe6514](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abe6514)
- Search results also mention Tennie, Bandini, van Schaik & Hopper 2020 (Biology & Philosophy), on the zone of latent solutions and ape culture, and Reindl et al. 2022, where young children spontaneously invented tool-use behaviours. **Neither was verified this session (no DOI checked).** — [search result: Tennie publications page](https://sites.google.com/view/claudiotennie/publications)

### Inferences (mapping to ACL)
- **The zone of latent solutions gives ACL a principled line between two kinds of skill:**
  - Techniques an agent could reinvent individually. Observation should only need to *raise the probability of reinventing* them, through stimulus or local enhancement.
  - Techniques beyond what an individual could reach. These require faithful copying of the process or teaching. Absent those, they can stay unlearned "in plain sight".
- **Suggestion.** Tag each technique in the nature layer with (i) its transparency and (ii) the number of hidden steps. Observation of transparent, low-step techniques mostly yields enhancement of individual exploration. For opaque, multi-step techniques, observation transmits little unless exposure is repeated and close, as with a kin or peer co-worker.
- This keeps culture emergent: whether a technique spreads depends on who happens to be near whom and on agents' own practice, not on a scripted plan.

### Gaps
- I did not verify the 2020–2023 zone-of-latent-solutions papers (Tennie et al. 2020; Reindl et al. 2022) or critiques of the concept. They should be checked before being cited.
- I did not retrieve recent ape over-imitation or "ghost" replications.
