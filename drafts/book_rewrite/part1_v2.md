# The World That Nobody Wrote — Part One (rewrite, in progress)

# Prologue — The Vessel

In the twelfth year of one world, a person named Reibik sat playing with whatever lay nearby: a piece of hide, a piece of bone, some fibre. Reibik tied them together, and what came out could hold water. Nobody had shown Reibik how. Nobody in that world had ever made such a thing, and in all the other worlds running beside it, nobody else ever would.

Reibik liked it and carried it everywhere. Reibik made a second one and showed the children how, and they showed others, and the way of making it passed from hand to hand for more than fifty years after Reibik died, changing a little each time, the way stories do.

For two years of that life, Reibik stood within reach of fresh water, holding a thing that could carry it.

Reibik never once filled it.

Not because Reibik couldn't. We can see what Reibik chose to do instead: gather food, give food away, talk, go looking for people. Filling the vessel was never near the top. In that world, the urge to carry water grows with how much thirst a person has known, and Reibik had always lived beside water. Out on the dry land, meanwhile, other people went thirsty. They had never seen a vessel.

The one holding the vessel was almost never thirsty. The thirsty had no vessel.

Is that small urge the whole reason? The record cannot say. It gives us the puzzle, not the answer.

Reibik never existed. Reibik lived inside a computer, in a world I built to answer one question: if nobody writes civilization into a world, will it ever appear on its own? Everything above happened, and every week of it was written down.

This book is about that question, and about the people who lived inside it. It is also about something harder to see: how easy it is, when you build a world, to slip the answer in without noticing, and how much work it takes to keep your own hands out of it.

# Before You Begin

This is a living book. My worlds are still running, and every new experiment adds to the story. When something I believed turns out to be wrong, I don't erase it. I write the correction next to it, so you can see how my thinking changed.

Everything in these pages happened in the record. Every name belongs to someone who lived in one of my worlds. Every failure is kept, because the failures taught me the most.

I wrote this book with AI as a tool, the same way I built the worlds.

# Part I — The Dream

## Chapter 1 — Why I Built a World

It started with JARVIS, my idea of a personal AI companion, inspired by Iron Man. I didn't want just another chatbot. I wanted something that knew me, could talk with me naturally, help manage my digital life, and become more useful over time.

But as I explored how to build it, I started asking bigger questions. What does it mean for an AI to truly understand someone? Can a system grow beyond what its creator programmed? And if I wanted AI to develop its own behaviour instead of following a script, how much would I have to decide in advance?

Before long I noticed something about almost every AI tool and simulation I looked at. Even when they looked intelligent, I kept wondering how much of what they did was really their own, and how much had simply been written in beforehand. Making one assistant smarter stopped being the question I cared about most.

I wanted to see what would happen if many people lived together in a world: met each other, shared what they knew, formed friendships and families, and learned from what happened to them. Not a world where I wrote the story, but one where the story came from them.

That is how ACL began: the Artificial Civilization Laboratory. The question changed from "How do I build a smart AI?" to "What kind of world would let intelligence and civilization grow on their own?"

The idea didn't come in one moment. It grew slowly, as I thought about intelligence and how complicated things come to be. Nobody designed a language. Nobody designed a city's markets or the first songs. They grew out of many people living, trying things and passing them on. Maybe simple rules and many lives can produce something far more complicated than anyone planned.

So I decided not to program religion, government, culture, or even the way my people talk to each other. I would build only the world and the people's nature: hunger, cold, fear, friendship, curiosity. Whatever came after that would have to come from them. The boundary between what I build and what the world makes for itself is the thing that fascinates me most. I write the laws of the world. I don't write its history.

A product is built to solve a problem you already understand. You know what it should do, and you make it do that better. ACL is built for questions I can't answer. Will my people find their own ways to communicate? Will traditions form? Will they help each other, fight, or make things I never imagined? I don't know. That is exactly why I want to find out. It may take a long time, and the answers may not be the ones I hope for. But even a failure can teach me how complicated behaviour grows out of simple things.

There was a second question underneath the first, and I gave it a name: Bob.

Bob is not a character. Bob is a question about one person. If someone were born inside a world like mine, learned from experience, made friends, kept memories, developed fears and habits, would that person be someone? Would they still be themselves if what they lived through were carried somewhere else, into another body, into our world?

Bob is the place I hope ACL leads. If we get there, we won't just have a simulation full of figures following rules. We'll have to face a much harder question: can someone born inside a simulated world truly become someone? And if we ever manage more than one, there will be Bobs.

I don't claim we are there. Nothing in this book shows that anyone in my worlds is conscious or feels anything the way we do. Bob is the destination. ACL is the journey toward finding out whether we can reach it. It is also why every person in my worlds is a particular someone, with a name and a history, never just a number in a crowd: if the question about Bob is ever going to mean anything, the first question has to be answered with people.

If you remember one thing from this book, I want it to be this: you can write the laws of a world without writing its history. But if you want to know what really grows there, you have to let the evidence argue with you, even about the world you built yourself.

## Chapter 2 — Games That Already Know the Answer

You have probably played a civilization game. You start with a few huts, you research pottery, then bronze, then writing, and a few hundred turns later you have cities, armies and a space programme. It feels like watching history unfold.

It isn't. Somebody decided long before you pressed "start" that bronze comes after copper and writing after pottery. When your little people discover bronze, nothing has been discovered. The game's designer discovered it, years ago, and wrote it on a list. A happiness meter that rises when you build a temple is not religion. A trade screen is not trade. A government picked from a menu is not politics.

These games are often beautiful, and they are honest about what they are: games. They never claimed to be experiments. The trouble starts when we treat them as if they could tell us something about how real civilizations began. If the outcome is written into the rules, the outcome can't prove anything. It is like hiding a coin in your own pocket and then being amazed that you found it.

There is a quieter version of the same trick, and it worried me more, because it is easy to fall into even when you are trying hard not to. Many simulations avoid lists of inventions but still reward the right answer. Their people are paid for inventing, paid for trading, paid for cooperating. Sure enough, inventing, trading and cooperating appear, and it looks like they grew on their own. But the reward was the recipe. The designer just moved the answer from the rules into the reward, where it is harder to see.

Real life doesn't work like that. Nobody paid the first person who tied a stone to a stick. Nobody awarded points for the first song. A good idea in the real world earns nothing until it happens to make someone's life a little easier, and even then most good ideas are forgotten.

So I set my world a harder test, and I have tried to keep it ever since:

- Nothing my people think with may contain a list of inventions.
- Nothing may reward an invention just for being an invention.
- Nothing may tell anyone what is useful.

If civilization appears under those conditions, it means something. If it doesn't appear, that means something too. Either way, I will have learned something real.

## Chapter 3 — What Would Count as Success

Before I could build anything, I had to be clear about what I was building, because it is easy to confuse with three other things.

A **game** is built to be played. Its rules exist to make your choices interesting. Whether its history is believable doesn't matter, and shouldn't.

A **simulation of one thing** follows many small actors under simple rules and watches what they do together: birds flocking, cars jamming on a highway, a disease spreading through a town. These are often excellent science. But each studies one thing, and the ingredients of that thing are put in on purpose. A model of traffic has cars and roads in it from the start.

A **simulation of history** models the big pieces: states, economies, wars, technologies. Those big pieces are what the model is made of. People, if they exist at all, are parts inside them.

What I wanted was none of these. My world has no player and studies no single thing. It has no states, markets or religions written into it at all. It has individual people, a physical world, and time. Anything bigger than one person, a family custom, a shared sign, a way of doing things, has to be built by people out of what they do, or it doesn't exist.

That kind of world may not even be possible. Finding out is part of the point.

So what would success look like? The word I kept coming back to was **open-ended**. A world is open-ended if it keeps producing things that are genuinely new: new kinds of objects, new habits, new traditions, not just new arrangements of the same few pieces. Life on Earth is like that. Human culture is like that, so far. Almost no simulation is. Most settle down after a while into a steady state where nothing new ever appears, like a snow globe after you stop shaking it.

I decided from the start to say in advance what would count as success and what would count as failure, so that I could never later call a failure a success just because I wanted it to be one.

Success, at its smallest, would be any culture at all appearing without being scripted: a way of doing things passed from one person to another, a mark that comes to mean something, knowledge that outlives the person who found it. At its biggest, it would be a world where new things never stop coming, where year five hundred holds things that year fifty couldn't have held.

Failure could come in three shapes, and I named each one so I would recognise it:

- **Nothing happens.** People live and die and never build up anything.
- **Something happens, but I put it there.** The result is real, but the experiment is worthless, because the answer was hidden inside.
- **Something happens, then stops.** Culture appears, settles, and freezes.

By October 2026 my worlds had clearly escaped the first failure, fought hard against the second, and walked straight into the third. Most of this book is about the third.

# Part II — The Rules I Gave Myself

None of the rules in this part were handed down at the start. Most of them were written after something went wrong. Each one is a scar.

## Chapter 4 — Nature, Not History

There is one rule underneath all the others, and every other rule is a way of keeping it: **I write nature. Nobody writes culture.**

I write the physics, the bodies and the feelings: what hunger is, how winter works, how flint breaks, what grief does to a person. I don't write tools, fire-making, shelters, farming, trade, leaders, feuds or myths. Those have to come from people living under nature's rules. In one sentence: I write the laws of the universe, not its history.

It works like the real world. Nobody gave the first humans a recipe for fire. They had bodies that felt cold, hands that could strike stones together, and eyes that noticed sparks. Fire came out of that, eventually. I give my people the bodies, the hands and the eyes, and then I wait.

This matters to me because I don't want to write the history of my civilization. I want to create the conditions in which history can happen. I can decide how much food a person needs, how memory works, how children are born, and how news can travel. Those are the rules of the world. But I don't want to decide what my people will invent, what they will believe, which traditions they will keep, or what kind of society they will build. If I put the answers in, I already know the ending, and I would only be watching my own decisions play back to me.

The obvious shortcut was a small list of inventions, just a few steps to get things started. I rejected it, because there is no safe size for a script. If the first step is written, every later step stands on it.

The rule is brutal in practice. My people start with nothing and may never get anywhere. It means long stretches of watching a world do very little. And it means the line between nature and culture has to be argued case by case. Is the fear of wolves nature or culture? My answer: being able to feel fear is nature. Being afraid of wolves in particular is learned, from being hurt, from seeing someone hurt, or from being told.

Two more rules grew out of this one.

**Nothing is heading anywhere.** My people have no purpose built into them beyond staying alive and the pulls of their own feelings. Nothing in them wants to invent, to advance or to build. Nothing rewards a world for becoming more complicated. If I nudged my worlds toward complexity, then finding complexity would prove nothing; it would be like tilting a table and being surprised that the marbles roll one way. My people do have curiosity, a pull toward the untried that comes from their temperament, the way some children take every toy apart and others don't. But curiosity is not a reward for being inventive. When people in my first long run made blades in their very first year, nobody had wanted a blade. Someone struck flint against flint, often while playing, and was left holding a sharp edge. The edge helped them gather food. Whether a blade ever appears in a world depends on whether that accident happens, and whether anyone feels the difference.

**No invention is special.** If my code treated some inventions as special, with their own rules or names, then those inventions were chosen in advance, by me. A rule for "spear" is a step on a hidden list even if no list is ever drawn. So every made thing in my worlds is just an ordinary result of the same physics. The part of the code that decides what people do contains no names of inventions at all, and a test reads the code and fails if words like "pot", "kiln" or "cooking" ever show up there. That test has caught us once already: during one of our big checks, an event had been given one of the forbidden words as a name, the test failed, and the name was changed. Names live only with the historian, a watcher who stands outside the world and, after the fact, calls a thing a raft or a blade by what it can do. Another test makes sure the historian never changes anything a person does.

We have found shortcuts in my worlds, places where people could use information they shouldn't have had. Those didn't make every result false, but they forced me to ask which conclusions my worlds could really support. If ACL only worked because I had secretly put the answers in, I would be disappointed. But I would rather discover that than spend years believing something that wasn't true. When we find a problem, I look at what it affected, keep the results we can still trust, fix the rules and run the experiments again. I don't erase the old results or pretend the problem never happened.

The goal isn't to make ACL look successful. The goal is to find out what it can really produce. **I want to program the possibilities, not the outcomes. I want to write the laws, not the history. And if the evidence shows I failed, I want to know.**

## Chapter 5 — A World That Doesn't Care

My world has no main character, and it protects no one. Rain falls where it falls. A storm puts out the fires of the careful and the careless alike. A band of five people on a small island, with no partner for anyone, dies out, and nothing steps in to save them.

That is how the real world works, and it's the only honest way to build one. A world that rescues the last survivors, or sends rain just as a people is dying of thirst, is a world with an author writing a happy ending. If I protected my people, every success they had would be partly mine.

The price is extinction. In my first long run, two of eight worlds died. In my thousand-year run, three of eight. In a run of twenty-four worlds a little later, after I had made the seas harder to cross, nine of the twenty-four were gone within two hundred years. These deaths are not bugs. They are what an uncaring world does. My job is to write them down and ask why, not to prevent them.

The world doesn't care, but it is consistent, and that consistency is where everything useful comes from. In my world, materials aren't items on a menu. Each is a bundle of physical qualities: how sharp it is, how long, how hard, how well it holds things, how warm, how well it floats, how tightly it binds, how soft it is. There are two ways to change them: strike one thing against another, or tie two things together. What a made thing can do follows only from its qualities. A sharp edge on a long handle helps with hunting. Something that holds helps carry food. Something warm helps in winter, if you wear it.

Nobody listed the raft. The raft exists because wood floats a little, tying two floating things together floats a bit better, and enough float lets a person stand on water. The first person to tie two sticks together made a raft, whether anyone meant to or not.

That is exactly what happened, and it taught me one of my most important lessons. My worlds start as islands, one founding band on each, so the peoples stay apart unless someone works out how to cross the water. The first question anyone asked of my first long run was: when will they invent the raft? The answer was almost embarrassing. Immediately. Wood is the commonest thing near any camp, and in every world someone tied two sticks together within the first three years, often within weeks, usually while playing. They had made a boat. Nobody knew.

And nobody used it. Almost every crossing between islands in that run happened without anything that floats, over narrow channels that my movement rules had quietly let people jump. Across open water, there were three crossings in nearly fifteen hundred years of world-time. In each world, dozens of people knew how to make a raft, and almost no one carried one. When we looked for the reason, we found three: nothing in the world ever showed anyone what floating was for; people carry only the few things they value most, and a raft was valued like a slightly awkward club; and the rare person who did set out to sea with one picked a random far shore, a different one every week.

The raft was invented and forgotten in the same moment.

The fix was not to reward rafts. That would have been writing history. Instead I changed the world. Water now really blocks walking. Far shores can be seen from the coast. And landing on new land became something you feel, the moment it happens, visible to anyone watching from the shore. After that, open-water crossings rose from almost none to anywhere between a handful and more than a hundred and fifty in a single world. There was a price, and it took two more experiments to see it: with the jump gone, many small bands were stranded on their islands, and stranded bands die. The change that let rafts matter made the world harsher for anyone who never built one.

The raft taught me a rule I have kept ever since: **a use has to be lived.** A thing isn't useful because I know what it's for. It becomes useful when someone in the world goes through something that matters to them because of it. Having a raft means nothing. Landing on a new shore with one means everything.

And that led to the question I now ask before adding anything new to my world: am I creating a condition people can use, or am I quietly scripting the result? Don't ask how to create trade; ask what conditions would make swapping things worthwhile. Don't ask how to create government; ask what pressures would make working together valuable. Don't ask how to make people fill vessels with water; ask what in their world could ever show them that a vessel holds water. When something I hope for doesn't appear, the temptation is always to add it. My rule is to add the condition, never the outcome, and if the condition isn't enough, find out why before adding anything else.

## Chapter 6 — Where I Fell Short

A book that boasts about its principles should also admit where it breaks them. Here are the places, known by October 2026, where my world falls short of its own rules.

The first is the words. When you watch my world on screen, people seem to talk to each other in English. They don't. What you see is my translation of what really passed between them: a warning, a piece of news, a lesson. My people have no language of their own yet. The marks they scratch into rock and bark are the only symbols that are truly theirs.

The second is a piece of knowledge I gave them by accident. A person carrying something that floats well enough will set out across the water toward land they have seen, and sometimes at random. Knowing that a floating thing is a way across the sea is exactly the kind of knowledge they should have to learn. A code review found it in October 2026, and it hasn't been removed yet.

The third is that the kinds of things that can pull at a person's mind are a fixed list: hunger, worry, a puzzle, grief, cold, and a few more. Goals can attach to particular things a person has found, but the kinds of goal can never grow. A real person can come to care about honour, or beauty, or a god. Mine can't, yet.

The fourth hurt the most when we found it, because it falls on exactly the inventions that cumulative culture is made of. Each person can remember how to make at most twelve things. A new one beyond that pushes out the one they value least, and a brand-new invention starts valued at nothing, so a full memory often loses a thing the moment it is made. Worse, when two recipes are valued equally, the tie is broken by the recipe's name, and the names of things built from other made things happen to sort first. So among equals, the more complicated invention is always the one thrown away. Nobody designed that as part of human nature. It was an accident of bookkeeping, found in October 2026 while we were counting what people keep.

The fifth is how narrow watching is. In real life, a child learns most things by watching. In my world, a person can work out how something is made by watching someone else in only two situations: seeing them land on a new shore with a raft, and hunting beside them. A carrier, a warm wrap, a blade used for gathering food or a piece of fired clay can be inherited, carried and lived beside for a lifetime without anyone ever learning how it was made.

The last two are about habits. My people learn their habits for a fixed set of situations; they can't invent a new kind of situation to have a habit about. And not all of their habits are learned from their own lives. Some are inherited at birth, as a softened copy of their parents'. Some are soaked up from whoever raises them. Some are picked up in conversation. Only part comes from their own experience. For a while the screen that shows a person's mind said how many of their habits they had "learned", as if every habit came from experience. That was wrong, and in October 2026 I changed it to show only what a habit is, not where it came from.

A history that records its principles but not its failures is just an advertisement. I'd rather you know where the cracks are.

# Part III — What Happened

From here on, this is the story of what my worlds actually did, in the order it happened. Before every experiment I wrote down what I expected, and locked it away where I couldn't change it. Many of those guesses were wrong. I've kept every one.

## Chapter 7 — The First Worlds Come Alive

The very first experiment was small and careful: ten people in two bands of five, living for a little over a year. I wanted to know one thing. Would my people do anything you couldn't explain as a simple reflex, like a thermostat switching on when it gets cold? I wrote down my guesses: that survival would come before everything else, that people would get good at predicting what foraging and resting would bring but bad at predicting each other, that friendships would feed themselves, and that the two bands would never meet.

I don't have the scored results of that first run. The guesses survived; the report didn't. I've left that gap as a gap, rather than fill it in from memory. What the first run did leave was a habit I have kept ever since: write the predictions down first, lock them away, and only then press "start".

The second experiment was the first time I let worlds run long enough to have a history. Eight worlds, up to two hundred years each. Twenty founders in every world, split into four bands of five, each band on its own island.

The worlds came alive almost at once.

In one of them, in the very first year, a person named Ruleidon went back to the place where they had left something, for the third time, to see if it would happen again. It was the first deliberate test of anything in that world: someone checking whether the world behaves the same way twice. In year four, a person named Tairo struck flint where dry things lay, and they caught. It was the first fire ever made by people in that world. Nobody had told Tairo that sparks make fire. Tairo had puzzled over sparks, and tried.

In year fourteen, in the same world, a person named Roukarail attacked a person named Kaileir. It was the first violence anyone there had done. It was a hungry year; three people starved after a poor growing season. Six years later, a person named Ludol attacked Kaileir. Kaileir fought back and killed Ludol. Revenge answered revenge, and a feud began between Kaileir's family and Ludol's.

Then the story of that killing began to travel. It went the only way anything travels in my worlds: parents telling children, neighbours passing on news, each person telling it as they remembered it. Eleven years after the killing, three people said it was someone called Rukeimei who had killed Ludol. Eighty years after, sixteen people believed it was Naikoubo. A hundred and eighty years after, fifty-one people believed it was Midigain. None of them had been there.

And yet the memory itself held. A hundred and eighty years after the killing, sixty-two people across five generations still remembered that Ludol had been killed. It was the longest-lived memory in all eight worlds. They had simply lost who did it.

That was the first time I saw my people carry history beyond living memory, which is the beginning of what a culture is. And in the very same story, I saw that what a people remembers is not the same as what happened. Think of a game of telephone, played across five generations. A people's past is something they make out of retellings. My world doesn't correct them, and shouldn't.

More of what I had hoped for showed up in those first eight worlds. Six of the eight survived. Every one of them developed shared marks, scratched into rock and bark, that came to mean water, shelter or a grave, more than seven hundred of them in all, and about a third of those marks reached people born after they were made. Myths, stories that weren't true but were told by several living people, appeared about twice a century in every world. In every surviving world, someone struck fire after puzzling over sparks. And every world had invented every kind of thing it was possible to make within a hundred years.

Some of my guesses were badly wrong, and those were the most interesting. Stories went wrong far more often than I expected: in one world, three quarters of what people believed about the past was false. And trust had nothing to do with truth. Whether a person was usually right made no difference to how much others trusted them. People trusted whoever fed them. It is not hard to think of human examples.

Then there was the raft, invented almost at once and almost never used, the story I told in Chapter 5.

So I made four changes, all at once. People would now earn their reputation by being right when it was checked, not just by being generous. They would have many more chances to check what they were told. The traces and marks left behind would be able to teach. And floating would become something felt, with the narrow channels between islands no longer jumpable.

Every change worked. People checked what they were told more than fifteen times as often. Reputation began to follow truth. People crossed open water in every world. In every surviving world, someone learned a technique from a mark another person had left, and in three worlds, a skill that nobody alive still knew came back because somebody read it from a mark.

And people started dying.

After a hundred years, my worlds held about forty percent fewer people than before. I had written down, in advance, that I would call the changes a failure if the population fell below half. It came within five people of that line. Half my predictions were wrong. The causes of death looked the same as before; births had simply fallen by a third. Nobody could say why.

So I stopped building and started digging. I replayed the first century of all sixteen worlds, the eight before the changes and the eight after, and checked that each replay matched the original exactly, the way you might watch a recording of a game frame by frame. Then I followed the path from a woman able to have children to a birth, step by step.

Before looking, I wrote down my guesses with odds. My money, at sixty percent, was on the islands: with the narrow channels closed, fewer people could reach a partner. The other main guess was that people were simply busier, with all the new things to check.

Almost every explanation failed. Women had children at almost exactly the same rate as before. People spent their time almost exactly as before. Babies went hungry about as often as before. One world showed my island explanation perfectly. There, almost nobody had a possible partner on their own island; the share of women with a partner halved, births fell to about a quarter, and the world dwindled away. But in the overall numbers, that one world was hidden among the others.

The honest answer was an uncomfortable one: eight worlds were not enough to tell a real effect from bad luck. It's like tossing a coin eight times and getting six heads. It might be a bent coin. It might just be chance.

So the next experiment was built to settle it, with twenty-four worlds, each run twice: once under the old rules and once under the new. I decided in advance that I would only believe the new rules lowered the population if they did so in at least seventeen of the twenty-four. They did in eighteen. The effect was real.

The cause was my island guess, but it worked in a way I hadn't pictured. Every world starts with four bands of five. Under the old rules, seven bands in ten eventually had a child with another band. Under the new rules, only four in ten did. And a band of five that never mixes with anyone almost always dies, under either set of rules. Within a few generations everyone on the island is related, and no one is left to pair with. Fewer than one isolated band in five still had a living descendant after a hundred years. The new rules hadn't made isolation deadlier. They had made it far more common: twice as many bands never met another band at all.

It cost more than lives. With fewer people mixing, fewer techniques were tried, and the ones that were tried were forgotten sooner. Fewer people keep fewer things alive.

I kept the four changes. Their good effects held in every world that lived. But I had learned two lessons I would meet again and again. A wall of water is a death sentence for a small people. And any change to the physics of a world can reach all the way into who gets born.

## Chapter 8 — A Thousand Years, and the Freeze

The experiment I had been waiting for was the long one. Eight new worlds, every rule locked, a thousand years each. I expected time to give culture room to grow. If a people had a thousand years, surely they would build something on top of what they started with.

My written predictions were cautious, and most of them came true. Three worlds died: two within their first fifty-five years, from hunger, thirst and children left uncared for, and one slowly, after four centuries, mostly from illness. Five reached year one thousand alive, each with somewhere between thirty-six and eighty-six people. For nine centuries their populations held almost flat, births matching deaths, like a village that never grows and never empties.

In almost every world, one people remained, and usually not a mixed one. In four worlds, everyone alive at year one hundred descended from a single founding band of five. The other three bands had left no one. The usual path to one people was not merging. It was one band outliving the rest.

One world proved me wrong in the most beautiful way. I had predicted there would be no separate peoples. In that world, two islands lay side by side for nine hundred and ninety years, each with between fifteen and fifty-three people. In a thousand years, not one person on either island formed a relationship of any kind with anyone on the other. They never met.

Their temperaments drifted apart early and stayed apart, while the way they differed kept changing: at first they differed most in how hard they worked, centuries later in how anxious they were and how much they cared for others. Their cultures grew apart in ways nobody designed. One island kept beliefs about leaving fibre, stone and wood at certain places in certain seasons, an old building practice. The other island never held any of those beliefs, in any year I checked, for a thousand years. The second island bound things with hide and worked bone; the first did neither. Their marks shared nothing: each island had made its own shapes for rabbits, for graves and for water. They were two peoples with two histories, across a stretch of sea, and neither ever knew the other existed. Think of two valleys in the mountains that grew their own dialects and customs for centuries without ever crossing the ridge between them.

Some things lasted the whole thousand years. The simplest carrier, fibre tied to fibre, was held in five worlds from year ten to year one thousand. About three in four of the people who came to hold it had been taught by someone. The rest found it again by themselves, because it is the easiest thing in the world to make.

But my predictions hadn't asked the question that mattered most, and the results answered it anyway.

Every kind of tool any of those worlds ever had, whether carrier, boat, club, blade, spear or warm wrap, first appeared within the first eleven years. After that, for nine hundred and ninety years, nothing new appeared. Not one new kind of thing.

Worse, the worlds slowly forgot. The number of ways of making things that people knew peaked somewhere around year fifty to a hundred, and by year one thousand it had fallen to about a tenth. Much of what was lost was the clutter of play. But not all of it. Spears were lost in every long-lived world, sometime between the second and fifth centuries, and never came back. Warm wraps were lost in all of them.

A world at year one thousand looked much like the same world at year thirty, and sometimes poorer.

It wasn't a bug. The experiment had worked exactly as designed, and that made it impossible to wave away. These worlds had history. What they didn't have was history that kept going.

The worlds went on. People lived their lives. Generations passed. But culturally, something had stopped moving. I had to face a hard possibility: building a world where people survive, talk and have children does not automatically build a world where culture keeps growing. Maybe my people lacked something they needed to invent. Maybe new ideas appeared and failed to spread. Maybe the groups were too small or too cut off to keep and build on what they learned. Or maybe the world rewarded staying alive without ever rewarding discovery.

I couldn't simply make my people invent more often and call the problem solved. That would be writing the answer in instead of understanding why culture had frozen. So the freeze became something to investigate, not something to hide. My question changed. I was no longer asking only whether a civilization could last a long time. I was asking what lets a civilization keep creating something new.

My first answer was the obvious one: maybe the world was too poor. With six materials and two ways of changing them, perhaps there simply wasn't enough to discover. So I made matter richer. Things now had weight, warmth, wetness, softness. Soft wet clay lay by rivers and lakes. People could hold something in a fire, fill something, and press soft stuff into a shape. I ran eight worlds, each for a thousand years, and predicted that in at least half of them, something new would still be appearing after year three hundred.

It didn't happen. In every world that lived three centuries, the last new thing appeared by year sixty-six. My people did take up the new physics, but only the shallow end of it. They shaped clay constantly, dozens of shapes in every world. And then they stopped. In all eight worlds, over up to a thousand years each, exactly one thing was ever changed by being held in a fire. Nobody ever filled a vessel. No water and no fire was ever carried anywhere. The richer worlds were also harsher, and I never found out why: four of the eight died out.

There was one small hopeful sign. In two of the surviving richer worlds, new traditions began after year one hundred and lasted a century or more; in one, a belief about leaving fibre at certain places in summer and autumn began around year three hundred and forty and lasted to the end of the millennium. No world under the old rules ever had a tradition that began that late. Two worlds against none is a hint at most, but I wrote it down, because it may matter later.

So I stopped building new worlds and started taking old ones apart. I replayed two of the richer worlds from their first week, with instruments that only read and never touch, and recorded every choice every person made, and every option they had weighed before making it. Before trusting a single number, I checked that the replays matched the saved worlds exactly. They did.

That is how I found the two people at the heart of this book.

The first was a child named Beimak, born in the world's eighth year to two of the founders. Beimak was curious, more than most, not very hard-working, not very sociable, quick-tempered and a little anxious. At about four years old, playing at a fire, Beimak held a dry lump of clay in the flames. The clay set hard. It was the first fired clay in that world, and as it turned out, the only fired clay that world would make in a thousand years: the whole history of pottery in that world.

Beimak remembered having made it, but valued it at nothing. Beimak never used it, never made it again, and never showed anyone. In the weeks that followed, Beimak's choices were ordinary ones: rest, gather stone, play, talk with an older sibling. Nine weeks after the firing, Beimak's mother starved to death. Three weeks later, Beimak's youngest sibling, not yet three, died of thirst. Two weeks after that, Beimak starved too. In five weeks the family had lost a mother and two children. Two weeks later, the world recorded that nobody alive knew how clay could be made hard in a fire. The knowledge had lasted sixteen weeks.

Beimak showed me that the physics worked: heat did exactly what it should. It showed how rare the chance was: in a whole century, adults stood at a fire holding a piece of clay that could be fired in only three weeks. And it showed something harder to see in tables. In my world, the people most likely to try something new are the ones who play, and the ones who play are children, who are also the ones most likely to die.

The second was Reibik, the person from the beginning of this book. Reibik was one of the founders of another world, very sociable, calm, not especially bold or curious, with a partner and six children. In the world's twelfth year, playing with materials, Reibik bound hide to bone with fibre and made the only watertight thing ever made in any of those eight worlds.

Reibik liked it and used it as a carrier the very next week, and again, and again, a hundred and four times, valuing it a little more each time. Reibik made a second one, and taught it: to one child, then another, then a third. The children carried the recipe on after their parent was gone. One of them taught it to a ten-year-old outside the family, who later taught it to their own child, and to a half-sibling. By then the recipe had changed in the telling: the later learners believed it was tied with hide, not fibre. The knowledge lived on for sixty years, until Reibik's child Baigi starved, and the next week nobody alive knew it anymore.

And for two years, Reibik stood within reach of fresh water, holding a vessel that could hold water, and never once filled it.

When I look at those two years, I see a question bigger than the vessel. The invention happened. The ability existed. But the act that would have made the invention matter never followed. Reibik forces me to separate things that look like one thing. Making a vessel is not the same as understanding what it can do. Understanding is not the same as remembering to use it. And remembering is not the same as choosing to act. Reibik's story alone doesn't tell me which of those failed. The record gives me the puzzle, not the explanation. But it showed me that I can't count an invention as the end of the story. I have to follow what happens next: whether it is used, whether someone else learns it, whether it survives, and whether it becomes part of life. **Reibik created the possibility, but the possibility never became a practice.**

The replays let me score every link in that chain, separately for fire and for water. Was there an opportunity? For fire, rarely, because fires are rare and brief. For water, constantly. Did anyone take the first step on purpose? Never, for either; the one fired lump came from a child's play. Did the material change as it should? Every time. Did anyone notice what it meant? Barely. Did it spread? The clay vanished in sixteen weeks; the vessel was taught for sixty years, but only ever as a carrier.

Before looking, the favourite explanation had been that knowledge was simply lost, that someone discovers a thing and it dies with them. Knowledge loss was everywhere in my worlds, after all. But that wasn't the main problem here: Reibik's vessel was taught to seven people and outlived its maker by decades. The problem was earlier. People met the opportunity and never took the first step. I gave that place a name: **the first-try bottleneck**.

One more suspicion had to be checked. Maybe my people never filled a vessel because the reward comes weeks later and far away, and they simply couldn't learn from delayed rewards. The habit part of their minds does forget fast: a reward five weeks after an act counts only a third as much. But I found two other ways they learn that reach much further back. Beliefs about leaving things at places learn from outcomes up to a year later. And how much a person values a thing they made learns with no time limit at all, as long as they still hold it: things first used more than a year after they were made were made again three times as often as things used at once. If Reibik had ever filled the vessel and drunk from it later, the lesson would have reached Reibik.

So the problem wasn't learning from delay. It was the first step itself, which no delay can teach, because it never happens.

## Chapter 9 — Slowly Forgetting

Once I knew the problem was the first step, I had to decide which side to mend: the world, or the people.

I could change my people: make them more curious, better at planning, more eager to invent. Or I could change the world: let nature take the first step by itself, the way crossing water once showed the raft its worth. I chose the world. I wanted to understand what could grow from the people I had already made. If they weren't inventing, I wanted to know why. Maybe their world gave them no reason to. Maybe useful knowledge couldn't spread. Maybe their groups were too small. Making them want to invent more might fix what I could see without answering what I wanted to know.

That isn't an absolute rule for me. Changing how my people think can be a fair experiment too, as long as I'm honest about what I changed. If I give them a new ability and their civilization blossoms, that doesn't prove they would have grown that ability themselves. It only shows what becomes possible under the new conditions. My default is to look at the world before rewriting the people. The point is never to protect my original people forever. It's to know which conditions lead to which outcomes, without pretending I discovered something I actually programmed.

So I let nature take the first step. Rain would fill any open watertight thing. Wading into a river or lake would fill it too. A fire hot enough would harden dry clay lying beside it. My people's minds stayed exactly as they were, not one line changed. Then, before any world ran, I went through the new code line by line against what I had promised to test.

I found six problems. One of them would have killed the experiment by itself. When a fire hardened clay, the recipe was stored under the wrong label, and the next time that person made a decision, the whole world would crash. Every test had passed, because none of them let a world keep running after a firing. The very first piece of fire-hardened clay in any world would have stopped everything, and I would have recorded the experiment as a failure for a reason that had nothing to do with the question. The other five were quieter, but each would have meant testing something other than what I had promised: rivers in my world can be waded, so they never counted as water and never filled anything; everyone standing near a fire had all their food treated as cooked; and so on. I fixed all six, wrote them down separately, and locked the fixes away before the first world ran. An experiment can be lost before it starts, quietly, inside one wrong label.

Then sixteen worlds ran for a thousand years each.

Half of the idea worked. Fires hardened clay in front of people more than six hundred times, where before it had never happened. People kept what the fire made. Twenty-five fired objects outlived the people who first carried them, and some were still being carried fifty years later.

The other half never had a chance. In eight worlds over up to a thousand years, rain or a river filled a vessel three times, and nobody ever drank water they had carried. The world can't fill vessels nobody makes.

And novelty still stopped. Across the eight worlds, more than six hundred lasting new things appeared in the first century, and exactly one after it.

That one late novelty excited me for a while. It appeared in year one hundred and fifteen, in the world where the two peoples never met, and it looked like a world that had almost found something new. Before building anything on it, I took it apart.

It wasn't new. It was a flint flake. The flake had first been made by a person named Daiyei, born in the world's first year. At four, playing, Daiyei struck flint against flint and was left holding a sharp edge. Over a long life Daiyei became a practised maker, with real skill in their hands, and died of old age in year sixty-two. The flake didn't die with Daiyei. It was taught at least twenty-four times, by several teachers, and read from marks on rock by people who had never met the one who made them.

Four generations later, a person named Mouthir, caring, fairly bold, and with almost no practice at making anything, took flint and struck a flake from a remembered recipe. Soon after, a curious forty-four-year-old named Vetibin did the same, and so did a child of eight named Sosei. All three were clumsy compared with Daiyei, and their flakes came out duller. The instrument I used to spot new things sorted things by how sharp they were, so it saw three people making a kind of flake nobody had made on purpose before, and called it the first lasting novelty after year one hundred in the history of my worlds.

It was the same flake, made worse. Mouthir starved fifteen years later.

The most hopeful number that experiment produced turned out to be a measure of loss. It's in this book because I almost believed it. And it taught me to check every "new" thing for this trap before reporting it: a decline can look like a discovery.

That raised a frightening suspicion. Maybe my worlds weren't frozen at all. Maybe they were slowly forgetting. So I went back through all sixteen thousand-year worlds and measured, every twenty-five years, how skilled their best makers were, how much the living still knew, and how often anyone made anything at all.

The worlds weren't frozen on a plateau. They were slowly unlearning.

Skill comes only from practice in my world. It's never taught, never inherited, and it dies with the person. The founders started with some skill and quickly got better: by year twenty-five, the best maker in each world was close to a master. After that, expertise fell in every single world. By year one thousand, the best maker alive was barely better than a beginner, about a tenth of what the founders had reached. The number of things the living knew how to make fell four- to eight-fold. And people stopped making things at all. In the first twenty years, each person made something on purpose two or three times a decade. By year five hundred, that had fallen forty- to sixty-fold. Even play-making by children fell about fifty-fold. In the later centuries, people almost never made anything.

Imagine a village of potters whose great-grandchildren still own a few pots but no longer remember how they were made, and whose children no longer play with clay. That was my worlds.

I then asked a sharper question: when someone carries, inherits or lives beside a useful thing, how often do they learn to make it, and how often do they actually make one? Teaching worked: recipes passed from teacher to learner well over a thousand times, and a couple of hundred more were read from marks on stone. But watching almost never worked. People watched others make or use things nearly as often as they were taught, and that produced exactly seven copies, all of them boats, because, as I said in Chapter 6, watching only counted at a raft landing or a shared hunt. For blades, spears, clubs, carriers and wraps, watching taught nothing at all.

Fired clay showed that knowing isn't doing. More than a thousand people carried or lived beside fired clay. A few hundred learned how to make it. And of the people who knew the recipe, nearly four in five never once made it on purpose. Knowing how was not enough when the thing solved no problem anyone had.

And the memory accident from Chapter 6 showed up in the numbers. When people made simple things in play, they almost always remembered how. When they made things built from other made things, they forgot one time in five, the moment they made it, five times as often. The parts a longer chain of invention would need were thrown away before anyone could build on them. That was a bookkeeping accident, not human nature, and I planned to remove it.

So the picture had changed. My worlds weren't stuck. They were falling slowly backwards, losing skill, losing knowledge and, most of all, losing the habit of making anything. The question became: why does nobody bother?

## Chapter 10 — Why Nobody Bothers

The next plan didn't come from me. Another AI working on the same project proposed something ambitious: seven experiments run together to test every remaining suspect at once. Free people to make things they already held. Let skill grow faster when practising beside someone better. Protect complicated recipes from the memory accident. Start worlds with eighty people instead of twenty. And every combination of them.

Its central idea was sound, and well put: skill must never simply be copied into a learner's head. That would be cheating. In my world, a person can only become skilled by doing. A good teacher can make each act of practice count for more, but the learner still has to strike the stone, the way an apprentice carpenter still has to plane the wood, however good the master is.

Before any code was written, I checked the plan against the code and the records, as I check every plan. I found five problems. One of its measures of success could never be met in runs that short. One experiment changed two things at once, so its result could never be explained. One promised identical maps while also making the map four times larger. And it underestimated, several times over, how long it would all take. Nothing was hidden or rewritten. The plan stayed exactly as written, and a dated correction was written beside it before any code existed. The correction also changed the order of work: instead of running all seven, run first the one that every other one depended on.

That one tested the most obvious culprit. In my world, a person holding a working tool won't make another while theirs is still good. Maybe that rule was stopping people from practising. So I loosened it: a thing that had proved its worth could now be made again, at half the usual pull. I decided in advance that if making didn't at least triple, I wouldn't read anything else into the result.

Making didn't triple. In the two worlds that lived, people made things less often, not more. The new rule added a little making early on, while the founders still valued their tools, and then making collapsed at the same pace as before. All six of my predictions were wrong. The honest conclusion is narrow: there was no sign that freeing people's hands helped. The rule had looked like the obvious culprit. It wasn't what was stopping them.

So I asked a simpler question. Did my people value anything they could make?

I looked at every adult, every fourth week, for a hundred and twenty years, in four worlds. The founders were the surprise. In the weeks when they could have made something, with both the know-how and the materials, they even considered making only one week in nine, and chose it one week in a hundred. Whatever they did instead was worth seven to nine times more to them. Making was never competitive, even for the generation that made the most. By the grandchildren's generation, adults held about one made thing for every five people, half of what they held was worth nothing to them, and making was considered in fewer than one week in a hundred and fifty. What won instead was everyday life: gathering food, talking, being with others, sharing food.

One number didn't fall. The best thing each person knew how to make stayed just as valuable to them, generation after generation. They knew how. They had the materials nine weeks in ten. They simply never had a reason.

Then I asked whether that reason existed in their world at all: not what my people thought their tools were worth, but what the tools actually did for them.

The answer embarrassed me. In my world, a week of gathering brings an adult nearly three times the food they need. The best tool adds about a fifth to a harvest that was never short. Across four worlds and more than five million units of food gathered, tools accounted for at most two percent of it. Most made things never paid back the food it cost to make them. Hunting, where a tool helps most, took up less than one adult-week in a thousand.

Imagine giving a family a tractor when they live in an orchard where the fruit falls into their hands. They wouldn't be foolish to leave it in the shed.

My people weren't being lazy. Their world gave them almost no reason to make anything. If that's true, much of what came before follows from it. Skill decays because nobody practises. Nobody practises because making is never worth more than daily life. Teaching recipes doesn't help, because knowing how was never what was missing. And a thing that solves nothing, like fired clay, is learned and then left alone. It also explains why the first decades looked so different. The founders made things in play, out of curiosity, rewarded with puzzles and surprises rather than food. When the surprises ran out, so did the making.

The surprising discovery wasn't that my people failed to invent civilization. It was that, in the world I gave them, technology had almost nothing to offer. Food was usually plentiful, water was near, and simple gathering met most needs.

Most of the time. Because there was one season when it didn't.

## Chapter 11 — Winter

One fact refused to fit. If food was so plentiful, why was hunger the biggest single cause of death in my long-lived worlds?

The answer was the oldest one in the world: winter. I followed every one of the nearly two hundred people who starved in four worlds back through the last two months of their lives. Seven in ten died in winter, and almost nine in ten in winter or the first weeks of spring, before the plants came back. In winter, one person-week in nine was spent starving; in summer, one in a hundred. Food is plentiful three seasons out of four, and the average hides the fourth.

Babies starved at seven times their share of the population. Of the children and babies who starved, almost every one still had a living carer. The carers were hungry too.

Two months before they died, in late autumn, the typical person who starved had no food on hand at all, and no food stored anywhere nearby. Not one of them was carrying anything that holds food.

It didn't have to be that way. In my world, loose food slowly rots, but food laid by in autumn still keeps more than half of itself through a winter. Seeds barely rot at all. A person can carry a winter's worth of food with a carrier, and most of it with bare hands. Storing food was possible, and it was exactly what carriers and heaps were for. But nobody ever filled even their bare hands. The one thing a carrier is really for, holding a winter's food, was never lived through, and so, as with the raft long before, it was never valued.

It was the raft again. The tool existed. Its use existed. Nobody ever felt it.

That left one question, and I could put it in a sentence: did my people fail to survive winter because they couldn't prepare, or because they didn't?

The difference mattered enormously. If autumn gave too little to spare, winter was just a fact of the land, and I would need to change the world. If autumn gave plenty and nobody kept any, the problem was in the people, in something they lacked that every farming people has.

I measured every adult, in every autumn, in four worlds: nearly twelve thousand autumns. All five of my predictions came true. Autumn gave plenty. An adult who gathered every autumn week could have laid by more than a whole winter's food. Ninety-eight autumns in a hundred offered plenty to spare.

Almost none of it was kept. Adults gathered in fewer than half their autumn weeks, because in my world the pull to gather grows with hunger, and in autumn they weren't hungry. The other weeks went to talking, sharing food and being with people. Nobody, in nearly twelve thousand autumns, entered winter with what a winter takes.

So the world allowed it, and the people didn't do it. Nothing in them looked ahead to a hunger that hadn't happened yet.

That mattered more than anything I had found before. Civilization is largely the doing of costly things now, because of problems that haven't happened yet. Granaries, fields, roads, bridges, reservoirs, savings accounts: all of them are of that kind. My people felt today's hunger, and not next season's.

For the first time, the evidence pointed at one specific thing my people lacked: the ability to feel, before it comes, a hardship they had lived through before. I was careful about how to build it. It must not say "store food". It must not mention carriers or heaps. It must not switch on because it is autumn. It must not tell anyone that winter comes. It could only take what a person had actually lived through and let it reach a little ahead. My people already had something like it for thirst: people who had been thirsty carried an expectation of thirst, learned from their own lives. So hunger got the same treatment. Each person learned, season by season, how often they had actually gone short, and the season they had gone short in was felt one season early, only in the pull to get food.

And I set one absolute safeguard: if anyone who had never gone short in a winter changed what they did in autumn, the experiment would be declared a failure, whatever else it showed. The safeguard held.

And the experiment failed its central test. Adults who had gone short the winter before gathered in autumn barely more than before. The typical person still walked into winter with almost nothing. Winter deaths didn't fall.

But something happened that had never happened in my worlds. In about one autumn in a hundred, a person filled their hands: a big store of food, where before there had never been one. Every one of them had gone short the winter before. They gathered in three autumn weeks of four, and walked into winter with six times what everyone else carried.

For the people it moved, the ability did exactly what I hoped. It moved too few of them. Nearly everyone had learned to expect a hungry winter, but for most people the lesson was faint: an average, the share of winter weeks they'd gone short, too weak to beat a week's ordinary business. Real people don't remember a hard season as an average. They remember its worst week.

Then I found out whose life a store actually saves. For any one adult, a single winter rarely kills. But I followed thirteen thousand child-winters, each beside the food their carer held when the winter began. When a carer started winter with very little, about one child in ninety starved before spring. When the carer held a few weeks' more, one in five hundred. A few weeks of food in a carer's hands made a child's winter five times safer.

That was the missing piece. The reward for laying food by is real and large. It just doesn't fall on the person who does the work. An adult who gathers through autumn mostly saves a child, not themselves, and a lesson learned only from one's own hunger will rarely teach that.

So I tried the strongest lesson a carer could learn: a child of theirs dying of hunger. And beside it a weaker one that could reach far more people: seeing someone die of hunger. The grieving did gather more the next autumn. Witnesses barely changed. And in no version did anyone walk into winter with more food than before. Then I read the deaths, last, as I had insisted. In one version, a third fewer children starved than usual. In another, a fifth more. Nothing upstream had changed to explain either; the carers held the same food. It was just worlds doing what worlds do. If I had read the deaths first, the version with fewer deaths would have looked like the breakthrough I'd been waiting for. Read in order, it was noise.

Before moving on, I wanted one more look back, at the hundred-odd autumns when someone, for the first time in the history of my worlds, filled their hands before winter. To me these were the first natural winter planners my worlds had ever produced, and I wanted to know everything about them.

There was nothing special about them. Their kindness, their hard work, their anxiety, their curiosity: all the same as everyone else's. What set them apart was only the lesson. They had gone short in a winter hard enough that the memory was more than twice as strong as most people's. They laid by a real store. Through the winter they gave almost a third of it away and ate the rest. Not one child in their care starved.

And then they stopped. Of the hundred and thirteen autumns in which someone planned for winter, only one was followed by a second.

Here is why, and it is one of the most important things my worlds have taught me. The planners stored food because they had gone short. Having stored, they didn't go short. And the lesson they acted on was learned from going short, so after a well-fed winter, it faded. The next autumn there was nothing left to anticipate, nobody laid anything by, and the hunger that had taught them came back. **A lesson learned only from suffering is erased by its own success.** I tested this before building anything on it, and it held: people who prepared went hungry far less, and afterwards their lesson fell to a quarter of its strength, far below the point where anyone prepares.

Human societies seem to have answered this with things that outlast experience. A story about a winter no one alive remembers. A custom of filling the store whether or not anyone is hungry. A granary that stays full because emptying it is forbidden. All of them keep the lesson when the suffering that taught it is gone.

So I asked whether stories could carry it. My people already told stories; that's how Ludol's killing survived a hundred and eighty years. Almost every death from hunger became a story, heard by dozens of people and still told decades later. But a death was remembered by who died and where, never by what of, or when. All that telling taught nothing about winter. So I made the smallest change I could: a story of a death would now carry that it was hunger, and in which season. Nothing about storing, nothing about what to do. Stories carry experiences, never instructions.

Forty thousand times, someone heard and believed a story of a death from hunger, with its season. Each time, they were moved a very little. People who had heard such stories gathered in autumn a few percent more. The number of people who filled their hands before winter rose from none to fifty-three. The effect was real. It was faint. A story teaches only as strongly as it is believed and as clearly as it is remembered, and almost nobody prepares until the lesson is strong.

Put together, these experiments showed a pattern. A lesson from one's own hunger, or from losing one's own child, is strong enough to make some people prepare, but it reaches only the few who suffered, and it fades as soon as preparing works. A lesson from seeing someone starve, or hearing that someone did, reaches far more people and outlasts any one person's suffering, but it is too faint to move anyone. In nothing I tried was a lesson both widespread and strong. The people who would prepare are not the people who hear; the people who hear are not moved enough to prepare.

Real peoples bridge this somehow: a lesson that grows stronger the more people hold it, a practice copied from those who prepare rather than a warning heard from those who suffered, a custom kept because it is the custom. My planners had shown that preparing was possible in my world. The next question was the obvious one. Did anybody notice them?
