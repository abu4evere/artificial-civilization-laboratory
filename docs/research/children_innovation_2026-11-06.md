## What the sources say (bearing on ACL)
- **Children make and use their own tools.**
  - In several forager societies, children use child-specific technologies to get food: Hadza children's sticky traps
    for weaverbirds, an activity adults do not do; Baka children's slingshots and small bows; Mikea children digging
    tubers adults ignore.
  - The review suggests pretend play may produce child-specific food-getting innovations (source 1).
  - **For CI1:** this supports LG, the law letting children make what they know. It is the same direction as the
    Lew-Levy et al. 2017 note in the morning report.
- **Complex tools come later.** Gidra children are given child-sized bows and are expected to work out how to make
  them; they make bows skilfully at about 14 (Nishiaki 2013, cited in source 1).
- **Young children are poor tool innovators in experiments.** Most children under 7–8 cannot invent the hook in the
  hook task, and 10–11-year-olds do not yet show mature use. This was replicated with settled San, Indigenous
  Australian and Ni-Vanuatu children (source 1).
  - The authors argue that the tasks hide children's real ability: the materials are unfamiliar, and the children
    are tested alone. In groups, 3–4-year-olds have innovated complex tools.
  - **A calibration question for ACL, not a finding.** In ACL, about 85% of world-new kinds are invented by children
    in play (RF1). Children aged 3 and over invent by recombining what they hold.
    - The experiments say young children rarely invent tools *on their own*. The ethnography says children make
      child-specific tools and toys, mostly learned from other children.
    - Whether ACL's child share is too high, and at what ages ACL's inventors are, is worth a read-only look. The
      inventor's exact age is not recorded by CI1's observer, only under or over 12.
- **Children learn tool-making mostly from other children.**
  - In observations of Hadza and BaYaka 3–18-year-olds, child-to-child teaching was about 75% of the teaching seen,
    even with adults nearby most of the time.
  - Tool manufacture, hunting and foraging knowledge are reported as learned "from and with other children" in many
    groups (source 1).
  - **For ACL:** CI1-C's new measure, kinds made by someone other than their inventor within 5 years, is the first
    look at whether a child's invention spreads. If it spreads, whether it spreads among children would be the next
    question.
- **Adolescents seek out innovations.** Among the Aka and Chabu, adolescents chose for themselves whom to learn new
  techniques from, and travelled far to good teachers (Hewlett, cited in source 1).
  - Riede et al. (from the summary) place a "sweet spot" for material innovation in late childhood or adolescence,
    before the costs of reproduction.
- **Autonomy in learning.**
  - Parents rarely interfere with young children's activities, even with knives or near fires.
  - Toy versions of adult tools are given without instruction ("opportunity scaffolding").
  - By 3, Nayaka children experiment with tools at will (source 1).
  - **For Bob:** this is an autonomy-relevant point. Children choose what to explore.
