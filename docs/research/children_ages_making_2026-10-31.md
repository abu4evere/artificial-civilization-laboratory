# Research note: at what ages do forager children use and make things? (31 October 2026)

**Why:** the creator, 31 October on Telegram, doubted ACL's flat age-12 limit: "in real life it differs like some
children can do something and others cannot… We gotta learn and research about that." In ACL, nobody under 12 can
choose `make`. Children 6–11 can forage (at 0.4 of an adult's rate) and play (`mind.py`; earlier note). **This note
collects what was actually read.** A source suggests; only a frozen study decides.

## What was read (labels per CLAUDE.md)
1. **Salali, G. D., Chaudhary, N., Bouer, J., Thompson, J., Vinicius, L., Migliano, A. B. (2019).** "Development of
   social learning and play in BaYaka hunter-gatherers of Congo." *Scientific Reports* 9:11080. PMC6668464.
   - **Read:** the abstract and the results paragraphs on tool use and teaching; **not** the time-budget tables by age.
   - "From infancy to early childhood, hunter-gatherer children learn mainly by imitating and observing others'
     activities. From early childhood, learning occurs mainly in playgroups and through practice."
   - **Using tools:**
     - "By middle childhood, BaYaka girls were already digging yams and using machetes efficiently."
     - A pair of 6-year-old twins went mushroom foraging on their own, knowing which to pick.
     - A 13-month-old was corrected while "imitating an older child when cutting an animal with a knife".
   - **Children differ by sex:** boys play more than girls throughout childhood. Girls forage wild plants from early
     childhood, and "the play-work transition [occurs] earlier for girls."
   - **Teaching** is mostly for social norms. Tool use, foraging and cooking are learned by observation, imitation and
     practice.
2. **Lew-Levy et al. (2022)**, 54 societies (read earlier today): children "readily manufacture their own toys", mostly
   miniature or full-sized versions of adult tools. **Making happens in childhood; that review gives no single age.**
3. **Lew-Levy et al. (2017)**, 58 publications (read earlier today):
   - infants are given toy versions of tools;
   - most subsistence skills are learned in early and middle childhood;
   - **complex, multicomponent tool making** is taught in adolescence;
   - innovation comes after adolescence.

## Bearing on ACL (suggestions, not proposals)
- **The evidence points to a gradient, not a cliff:**
  - simple making (toys, copies of adult tools) in childhood, through play and practice;
  - complex multicomponent making later, in adolescence, when it is taught;
  - with differences between children (by sex in the BaYaka data).
- **A law closer to this** would not move the age-12 line. It would let making start in childhood, with ability rising
  by skill or age (ACL already has a crafting skill that grows with play). Composite making could stay harder.
- **This is a nature change** (Law 126; the creator's decision, or the safe path in `CLAUDE.md`).
  - **Evidence first:** the time-budget tables by age (Salali 2019) and an age-graded source on tool making are still
    to be read.
  - No frozen study is drafted.

## Addendum (1 November 2026): Salali et al. (2019), the time budgets
**Read now:** the results paragraph on time allocation, Table 2 and the figure 3 caption, the video-sample age groups
(Table 3), and the discussion's first paragraph. The figures themselves (images) were not seen.
- **The time budgets measure play and childcare/domestic work, not tool making.** So they give **no age for making
  things.** The gap noted above stays open.
- **The shift from play to work is gradual and differs by sex:**
  - **girls:** play time "decreased after 5 years of age", as childcare and domestic work grew;
  - **boys:** play stayed "around a third of their in-camp hours" between ages 5 and 12, then "declined continuously
    throughout the teenage years";
  - both reach "a similarly low level upon reaching adulthood".
  - Table 2: play falls with age, more steeply for boys (age × sex term); N = 96 rows; children with under 10 scan
    observations were excluded.
- **Practice comes early:** children "employed practice-based learning at all ages, but their efficiency at tasks such
  as using tools was low in infancy". Once they learn a skill's basics by watching and imitating, "they start
  practicing those skills from early ages".
- **Bearing on ACL** (a suggestion only): this supports the gradient reading again. Ability is low and rises with
  practice from early childhood, and the move from play to work differs between children. No single age line is
  supported by anything read so far. **Still missing:** an age-graded source on tool *making* itself.
