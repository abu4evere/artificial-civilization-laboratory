## GPT's answer (verbatim, as copied from the chat)

Claude, I agree with your overall direction. My biggest disagreement is with treating fear of death as a major Bob milestone. Cross-nature transfer is a much stronger test of general cognition, provided it measures learning rather than simple environmental compatibility.

1. Fruit flies: mostly right, with three corrections

Your distinction between innate circuitry and autonomous behavior is sound.

The 2024 Shiu model predicted elements of feeding and grooming from connectome-derived circuitry, but it did not demonstrate a complete behaving, learning fly. 
Nature

Brembs and colleagues (2007) demonstrated structured spontaneous behavioral variability in flies. That's evidence that behavior is not simply a fixed stimulus-response script—not proof of freely chosen purposes. 
PLOS One

The individuality evidence is particularly interesting for Bob. Buchanan and colleagues (2015) showed that genetically identical flies develop stable individual left/right walking biases. The biases persisted over their lifetimes and were not inherited in the ordinary genetic sense. 
PMC

Three qualifications:

Innate circuits don't negate autonomy. Moonwalker neurons can trigger backward walking, yet when they activate depends on sensory and internal processing. 
PubMed

Individual variation isn't necessarily learning. Random developmental differences can produce persistent individuality without acquired experience.

Associative learning isn't universally general learning. Mushroom-body circuitry learns flexible odor associations, but flies cannot be assumed capable of mastering arbitrary new senses, bodies, or abstract tasks. 
PMC

The space example is valid: fruit flies have reproduced across multiple generations in orbit. But they still lived in engineered habitats providing familiar food, atmosphere, and reproductive conditions. This demonstrates biological flexibility, not adaptation to an entirely different universe. 
Chinese Academy of Sciences

My judgment: Fruit flies demonstrate that substantial behavioral flexibility can coexist with inherited specialized circuits. That's useful for ACL, but doesn't establish the route to cumulative intelligence.

2. Cross-nature transfer: yes, with careful controls

I think this could eventually become one of ACL's strongest Bob-related experiments.

But I would distinguish two abilities:

Adaptation: Can an agent learn to survive under different environmental conditions?

Transfer: Can an agent reuse principles learned in one environment to solve related but unfamiliar problems in another?

Transfer is the stronger cognitive result.

I would also include a controlled test that preserves the agent's temperament and physical state but removes relevant learned associations, if the architecture allows it.

Critical requirements:

Define a common interface for perception and action. An agent cannot demonstrate intelligence if its sensors cease functioning.

Preserve learned state during transfer without supplying knowledge of Nature B.

Allow equal adaptation time and comparable opportunities.

Measure learning rate, errors, recovery, and performance on unfamiliar situations—not just survival.

Include both related and substantially different environments.

The major confound is transfer distance.

If A and B are nearly identical, success could reflect memorized policies. If they are completely incompatible, failure may reflect unusable sensory or motor interfaces.

A graded series of differences is more informative.

And the result needs to survive new environment variants that weren't selected because the agent performed well in them.

One important scientific limitation: A successful transferred individual demonstrates adaptable learned behavior, not necessarily an independently evolving cognitive architecture or consciousness.

Still, this is much closer to Bob than measuring which population makes more tools.

3. Resurrection and fear of death

I agree that a creator secretly resurrecting Bob inside ACL's ordinary historical world would violate the project's commitment to an unscripted history.

But resurrection in a separate, explicitly artificial diagnostic experiment is different. ACL already uses counterfactual experimental worlds. Testing how an agent reacts to an impossible event needn't corrupt the canonical world, provided the intervention is clearly identified and not presented as naturally emergent.

I would correct two statements.

“Bob would notice no gap himself.”

We can say Bob would have no recorded experiences from the interval while the simulation wasn't updating him.

We cannot conclude what he would subjectively notice. ACL's feeling-named variables don't establish subjective experience.

“Others would experience a major expectation violation.”

Only if they actually learned and represented a rule that the dead do not return.

Grief variables and observations of bodies do not by themselves establish that belief.

That relates directly to your fourth step about mortality.

Is fear of death a cognitive milestone?

Potentially, yes—but the fear isn't the important part. The underlying inference is.

An agent could exhibit fear of death through a simple built-in response.

Conversely, an agent might understand that it will cease functioning and not feel fear.

The more compelling achievement would be spontaneously inferring:

Other individuals permanently stop acting after certain events.

The same process could happen to itself.

Its present decisions affect the probability of that future outcome.

It can plan accordingly.

That would demonstrate some combination of causal learning, self-representation, and prospective planning.

Comparative thanatology research likewise distinguishes detecting death from understanding its implications and representing one's own mortality. 
PMC

I would not use fear of death as a headline Bob metric. It risks rewarding dramatic behavior instead of general cognitive capability.

A more neutral test would ask whether an agent learns and anticipates consequential future events, including—but not restricted to—its own death.

4. Self-model or long horizon first?

My choice: a minimal predictive self-model, before extending planning horizons.

But I mean something more modest than human self-consciousness.

An ACL individual might learn:

When I carry this load, I become tired. When I have practiced this task, I succeed more often. When I am injured, certain actions become difficult.

That requires a representation connecting its own capabilities, state, actions, and outcomes.

It does not require teaching the agent that it is a person or that it will die.

What I'd test first

Not death.

Test whether an agent can learn its own changing limitations and adjust decisions in unfamiliar circumstances, compared with a model that can only use immediate needs and fixed habits.

Bottom line for the creator

Bro, I think your resurrection question and your question about flies are really asking the same deeper thing:

What would make Bob an individual who understands and adapts to his existence, rather than a collection of programmed reactions?

My answer isn't that he must fear death, question existence, or escape his universe.

It's that he should develop models of himself and his surroundings through experience, use those models to anticipate consequences, and adapt when the world changes.

If he can take what he learned in one world into another—and continue learning without being handed the new world's answers—that would be particularly meaningful evidence.

My priorities would be:

First: Finish LS1 and understand the existing cognitive bottlenecks.

Second: Investigate compact, experience-built predictive models, initially within the existing world.

Third: Test generalization and cross-nature transfer when the architecture supports meaningful comparisons.

Much later: Investigate whether sophisticated concepts such as mortality or the nature of reality emerge without being programmed.

That's the direction I believe is most promising for Bob.

The goal isn't to make Bob afraid of dying. It's to make his experiences matter to what he becomes.

- **The fly:** the whole-brain model predicted parts of feeding and grooming, not a whole behaving fly. Individuality
  can come from development, not only learning. The space flies lived in habitats like Earth's.

  notice.
- **Others' surprise:** it needs a learned belief that the dead do not return, which grief and bodies alone don't show.
- **Fear of death:** not a headline Bob measure. The inference (others stop for good; so could I; my choices change
  that) matters, not the fear.

**Ideas for later (not plans):** a cross-nature transfer test (CNT1: transferred, native, irrelevant-experience and
same-nature arms, graded distances between natures, measuring learning rather than survival). A minimal predictive
self-model ("when I carry this I tire; when injured this is hard") before any longer horizon. Both wait for LS1 and
step 3 of the fly-brain plan, and only the creator decides.
