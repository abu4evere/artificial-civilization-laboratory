> Bro, my recommendations are 1(b) and 2(b). Both protect ACL's core principle: change nature only when the evidence
> justifies it, and don't confuse one measured improvement with overall success.
>
> 1. LM1 — Parts-first teaching. Recommendation: (b) Adopt L1 only after a frozen extinction check.
> Why: The transmission result is strong: +2.15 percentage points, with a confidence interval of +1.7 to +2.6 points and
> Holm-adjusted p ≈ 4 × 10⁻¹⁷. The intervention has a plausible real-world analogue in how human children learn tools and
> skills through simpler tasks and component parts. But the study did not establish that L1 preserves survival. L had 93
> extinctions versus 79 in control, and ML had 86. Those descriptive counts do not prove harm, but they raise a
> legitimate question.
> Next step: Freeze a narrow, preregistered comparison of L1 against control, using matched starting worlds and a
> prespecified extinction criterion. Keep the intervention and every other rule unchanged. Do not alter nature v3 until
> the check is complete.
> Main risk: The extra teaching improves transmission while unintentionally making the civilization less likely to
> survive—or the check produces an ambiguous result because extinction is noisy.
> Decision principle: A strong transmission result justifies investigating adoption, not automatically adopting the rule.
>
> 2. Embedded food — FX2/FX3. Recommendation: (b) Run a read-only study of how tool use is learned and spreads in FX3's
> saved worlds. Do not adopt embedded food into nature.
> Why: FX3 shows that the tool route increases tool use: +9.3 percentage points in S25 and +2.2 points in B50. It does not
> show a meaningful survival benefit. Both survival-effect estimates are +3.1 percentage points, with intervals spanning
> possible benefit and harm. Every embedded-food arm had substantially more extinctions than control: 20–41 percentage
> points more, depending on the arm. The current evidence therefore supports the narrower conclusion that the tool route
> changes tool use—not that embedded food or tools improve survival.
> A read-only study can investigate who learns to use tools, from whom, how quickly the behavior spreads, whether use
> persists, and where transmission fails. Use the saved worlds and preserve the existing results; do not change the
> simulation or treat descriptive patterns as causal proof.
> Main risk: The audit finds interesting patterns that are mistaken for evidence that tools cause survival differences.
> Tool use, tool possession, and survival must remain separate outcomes.
>
> Decision summary: LM1 parts-first teaching — 1(b): Frozen extinction check first — Strong transmission benefit,
> unresolved survival concern. Embedded food — 2(b): Read-only learning-and-spread study — Tool use rises, but survival
> [does not; truncated in the window].
