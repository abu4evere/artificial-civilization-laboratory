Questions about the Artificial Civilization Laboratory

What is the Artificial Civilization Laboratory (ACL)?

The Artificial Civilization Laboratory (ACL) is an agent-based simulation of hunter-gatherers in which nature is programmed and culture is not. Simulated people have bodies, needs, kinship, feelings and materials to work with, but no list of inventions, no reward for inventing and no language model. ACL tests whether culture and civilization can emerge from nature alone, and what stops them when they do not.

Who built ACL?

ACL is built by Abdurashid Khudoyberdiev. The code and every experiment record are on GitHub; the story of the project is told in the book The World That Nobody Wrote.

Does ACL use large language models?

No. A language model trained on human writing already knows what a village, a trade or a law is, so if its agents built one it would be impossible to tell emergence from recall. ACL's people learn by trial and error and from each other, inside the simulation.

What has ACL found so far?

About nine in ten useful inventions are never learned by anyone except their inventor; many are lost from a limited memory before any chance to teach. About a third of small worlds (20 founders) die out, not from famine or war, but because too few people find a partner. Teaching a learner the missing part of a complex invention after a failed lesson raised how much was passed on; fixing an accident in how memory chose what to forget did not.

How does ACL keep its results honest?

Every study's plan and prediction are written down and frozen in the repository before any code or data. Hundreds of worlds are compared side by side, the simulation replays exactly, instruments are checked to change nothing, and wrong predictions are kept in the record.

Is ACL a model of real human history?

No. ACL is a model. It produces candidate explanations precise enough to test, compared with evidence about real foragers where possible, but it does not show how real societies worked.

Is ACL open source?

The code is public on GitHub so anyone can read it and follow every experiment. It is not under an open-source licence: all rights are reserved by the author.
