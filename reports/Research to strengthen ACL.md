# Connect the bands before tuning the minds

ACL's three bottlenecks share one root cause that the literature names precisely: each ACL world is a single, closed band of 20–40 people, and every body of evidence examined here (demographic viability models, forager ethnography, cumulative-culture theory, lab transmission chains) says such a unit cannot sustain either a mating pool or a complex culture on its own. Real foragers live in metapopulations of roughly 500–1,000 people spread over 18–50 bands; they find spouses across band lines; and over a lifetime a man watches more than 300 other men make tools, against about 20 for a chimpanzee ([Hill et al. 2014, FT](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0102806)). So the first remedy is structural and evidence-anchored: found worlds as several bands on a realistic landscape and let visiting, marriage and mixing arise from existing drives. Do not make partner search easier inside a band. The second remedy is measurement before mechanism. ACL should log each learner's effective pool of models, the social versus individual share of every skill, technique persistence and lineage depth, and contact rates between bands, so that "the duller flake" and the "freeze" become quantities that can be tested against Henrich-style threshold models rather than vague symptoms. The third bottleneck, storage and sowing being shared away, is not a bug: it matches the ethnography of mobile foragers, who give away 70–90% of large packages ([Gurven, Hill & Kaplan 2002, FT](https://gurven.anth.ucsb.edu/sites/secure.lsit.ucsb.edu.anth.d7_gurven/files/sitefiles/papers/gurvenetal2002JAR.pdf)). The honest response is to build the *conditions* under which real foragers stored (cold or strongly seasonal worlds, mass-harvestable gluts, the ability to choose co-residents or leave), then see whether restricted sharing emerges. Finally, ACL's existing rigour (frozen predictions, holdout seeds, sham arms, hash-verified observers) is already ahead of most agent-based modelling. Its gaps are a pre-declared set of empirical patterns, ablation arms for each emergence claim, effect sizes with smallest-effect thresholds, and global sensitivity analysis.

**How to read the citations.** Every source carries the reading label assigned in the research notes. **FT** means the open full text was retrieved and the relevant passages were read; often this was through keyword extraction or an automated summariser, so not every word was read. **ABS** means only the abstract was read. **REC** means only the bibliographic record was verified; any content claim comes from prior knowledge or a search snippet and is unverified. **PRE** marks a preprint that has not been peer-reviewed. Where the notes disagreed on a label for the same paper, the stronger reading is used and noted. Citation corrections and unverified items are gathered in a ledger near the end.

## A closed band of 20 is below every demographic and ethnographic benchmark

### The extinctions are what the evidence predicts, not a calibration error

About a third of ACL worlds die out because people cannot find partners. The demographic literature says this is the expected fate of an isolated group of that size. A non-spatial agent-based model by White found a demographically viable population of about 150 people against random swings in mortality, fertility and sex ratio. Less restrictive marriage rules made small populations more viable ([White 2017, FT via automated page summary](https://doi.org/10.18564/jasss.3393)). Per that summary, populations under about 40 were not viable over 400 years, and 40–50 survived only under the most permissive rules. Those small-population figures came through an automated summary and must be checked against the paper before ACL quotes them. Wobst's classic Monte Carlo work on the minimum size of Palaeolithic mating networks is the older anchor for the same question, but its numbers were not verified ([Wobst 1974, ABS](https://doi.org/10.2307/279579)).

Field data show why. Across 32 present-day foraging societies (5,067 individuals), the mean experienced band size was **28.2 adults**; either sex may disperse, and most co-residents are genetically unrelated ([Hill et al. 2011, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1199071)). (The cumulative-culture notes held this paper as record-only; the forager notes read the abstract, which contains the "mostly unrelated" finding.) Among the Savannah Pumé, two study bands averaged only about 10–12 adult men and 10–12 adult women each. The adult sex ratio swung from **0.67 to 0.43** (proportion male) from year to year, driven mostly by the sex ratio at maturity rather than by deaths. **15% of first marriages and 37% of later marriages involved relocating** ([Kramer, Schacht & Bell 2017, FT, figures extracted via a fetch tool and needing a spot-check](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5540858/)). The authors' conclusion fits ACL exactly: in small populations, finding a mate means looking outside one's local group. Marriage among foragers is overwhelmingly a matter between groups. Arranged marriage occurs in **85%** of hunter-gatherer societies in the sample, brideservice or bride-price in **80%**, and arranged marriage is unrelated to latitude, mobility or density ([Walker et al. 2011, FT](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0019066)). The authors infer a "regulated exchange of mates" through alliances spanning several residential communities.

A further constraint makes some die-off realistic. Across 227 Amazonian populations, population volatility declines with size as N^−1/4 rather than the N^−1/2 expected if individuals were independent. Shocks are synchronised, so small populations face higher extinction risk than classical viability analysis predicts ([Hamilton & Walker 2026, FT](https://doi.org/10.1017/ext.2026.10013)). ACL should therefore not treat every extinction as a bug. The right comparison is between extinction rates at 20 founders and at a realistic metapopulation of several hundred, under the same correlated shocks.

### Real foragers live in fluid bands nested inside large, mixing networks

ACL's band is far more closed than any measured forager group. **78% of ACL's co-resident adults are still together after two years**, whereas Ache band composition "was stable over periods of months but not years" ([Hill et al. 2014, FT](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0102806)). Among the Martu, residential and foraging groups are small but drawn from fluid, large-scale networks dominated by non-relatives ([Bird et al. 2019, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhevol.2019.03.005)). Hill et al. 2014 give the best quantitative anchor. About 560 Northern Ache lived in 18–20 bands, usually 10–30 km apart, across a core range of roughly 5,270 km². About 950 Eastern Hadza lived in 49 bands, a mean of about 35 km apart. For random pairs of same-sex adults *in different bands*, common activities such as conversing, sharing a camp or food, or watching someone make a tool happened at about **0.20–0.30 per pair per year among the Ache**, and 2–5 times less among the Hadza. Rare activities such as hunting together, giving gifts and lending tools occurred at 0.02–0.08. Ritual ties covered about 20% of Ache pairs and 16% of Hadza pairs, and a shared Hadza ritual raised interaction rates in all 13 categories. Agta proximity sensors show the same multilevel shape. In the forest group, 59% of possible within-camp pairs interacted against 28% of between-camp pairs; households were mostly close kin, and links between camps were mostly non-kin ([Migliano et al. 2020, FT](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aax5913)). Dyble and colleagues' agent-based model, calibrated on Agta and BaYaka co-residence data, shows why camps contain so many non-kin even when everyone prefers kin: men and women both have a say in where to live ([Dyble et al. 2015, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaa5139)).

### Movement should be daily forays from camp plus rarer camp moves, not a random 3-tile step

ACL's fixed 3-tile random step contradicts the best GPS data on forager movement. From 2,078 person-days of Hadza GPS tracks, women walked **7.6 km/day** and men **12.9 km/day**. Men covered about **3.9×** the area women did. Outbound paths were more winding than return paths (sinuosity 1.55 out versus 1.50 back for men, 1.44 versus 1.38 for women), which implies navigation by memory of camp location. More than 95% of forays were to obtain food, water or firewood ([Wood et al. 2021, FT](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC8060163)). A follow-up on the same sample found that people spent 25% of their time within 24 m of the hearth and 17% of their time more than 2.1 km away. It also found that a few camp members "leave on short visits to neighbouring camps", but gives no rate ([Wood et al. 2024, FT](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC11449209)). Residential moves are separate, rarer events. Compiled intervals include Ache 6.3 days, Punan 8.1 and Hiwi 9.1, and a demand-sharing model holds for moves 5–30 days apart; these per-society values are secondary compilations ([Lewis et al. 2014, FT](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC4284614)). Batek groups left camps when perceived returns fell, as the marginal value theorem predicts, after long deliberation ([Venkataraman et al. 2017, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1617542114)). Annual residential travel averages roughly 158–174 km, and distance per move scales with climate and density ([Hamilton et al. 2016, FT](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC5094510)). A search snippet attributed move intervals of 5–10 days (Ache), 10–15 (Hadza) and 63.4 (!Kung) to Kelly 2013; this is unverified until Kelly's Table 4-1 is checked ([Kelly 2013, REC](https://dn710205.ca.archive.org/0/items/robert-l.-kelly-the-lifeways-of-hunter-gatherers-the-foraging-spectrum-cambridge/Robert%20L.%20Kelly%20-%20The%20Lifeways%20of%20Hunter-Gatherers_%20The%20Foraging%20Spectrum-Cambridge%20University%20Press%20(2013).pdf)).

On Lévy walks, the evidence argues against hard-coding them. Hadza foragers showed Lévy-like walks in "nearly one-half of all foraging bouts" ([Raichlen et al. 2014, ABS; fitted exponent not verified](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1318616111)), but on a modest sample of GPS tracks from four camps ([Wood et al. 2021, FT](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC8060163)). Rural farmers collecting firewood show the same signature about half the time, with exponents near 1.7 ([Reynolds et al. 2018, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0199099)). When the move-length distribution is left free to evolve, searchers evolve *intermittent* search: frequent local moves plus rare long ones. That beats Lévy walks and, when it interacts with resources, can look like one ([Campeau, Simons & Stevens 2024, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1086/729220)). Encounter-conditional, area-restricted search outperforms Lévy flights whenever foragers know something about where resources are ([Ross, Pacheco-Cobos & Winterhalder 2018, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jtbi.2018.07.031); see also [Garg & Kello 2021, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-84542-w)). For ACL, Lévy statistics belong in the list of emergence tests, not in nature.

### Suggestions for bottleneck 1 that respect ACL's rules

The founding population is an initial condition, and the evidence-anchored choice is several bands, not one. A world founded as, say, four to eight bands of 20–40 people sharing ancestry and kin ties, spaced 10–35 km apart inside a 5,000–15,000 km² range, matches the Ache and Hadza baselines. To respect "never tuned toward survival", this change must be justified by the ethnographic numbers above and frozen in a new pre-registration *before* any run. The single-band, 20-founder configuration should stay as a comparison arm, and extinction rates should be reported as an outcome, not used to adjust the layout. Movement should become central-place daily forays at a declared tile scale that is checked against 8–13 km/day. Residential moves should be triggered by local depletion, so that intervals emerge and can be compared with 5–30 days.

For mate search, the evidence points to *capabilities* rather than norms. Agents need a way for information about unmarried people in other bands to reach them through kin, and a way for visiting to arise from existing drives: wanting a partner, wanting food, and attachment to kin who live elsewhere. That is enough for alliances, kin brokerage and relocation to emerge or fail on their own. ACL should not script arranged marriage or exogamy. The emergence tests, frozen in advance, would be:

| Emergence test | Empirical target | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Adult band size | about 28 adults | Hill 2011 (ABS) |
| Within-band relatedness | most co-residents unrelated | Hill 2011 (ABS); Dyble 2015 (ABS) |
| Band membership persistence | stable for months, not years (versus ACL's 78% at two years) | Hill 2014 (FT) |
| Annual between-band contact, random adult pairs | 0.20–0.30 (Ache); 2–5× lower (Hadza) | Hill 2014 (FT) |
| Year-to-year swings in band adult sex ratio | 0.43–0.67 proportion male in Pumé bands of about 20 adults | Kramer 2017 (FT, spot-check) |
| Marriages involving relocation | 15% of first, 37% of later marriages | Kramer 2017 (FT, spot-check) |
| Residential move interval | about 5–30 days in tropical settings | Lewis 2014 (FT, secondary values) |
| Daily travel | about 8 km (women) and 13 km (men); return paths straighter than outbound | Wood 2021 (FT) |

## Cumulative culture is lost when the effective learning pool is small and closed

### The treadmill models predict ACL's "duller flake"

In Henrich's model, every copy loses skill on average (a parameter α, reflecting how hard the skill is), with a spread β around that loss. Learners copy the most skilled model they can see, and the expected gain from choosing the best of N models grows roughly with ln N. That creates a critical *effective* population size, "the size of the interacting pool of social learners", below which complex skills decay while simple ones persist. The Tasmanian loss of bone tools, nets and spear-throwers over about 8,000 years was the motivating case ([Henrich 2004, ABS](https://doi.org/10.2307/4128416)). The Tasmanian interpretation is contested ([Read 2006, REC](https://doi.org/10.2307/40035327)). A robustness analysis found that Henrich's model "largely withstands" testing, but Powell et al.'s variant fails when copying is less selective. Whether real skill distributions are Gumbel, logistic or normal remains "an open empirical question" ([Vaesen 2012, FT](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0040989); [Powell, Shennan & Thomas 2009, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1170165)). When each newborn copies the best of only a few acquaintances, the innovation rate matters more than population size ([Kobayashi & Aoki 2012, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tpb.2012.04.001)). Social connectedness affects the equilibrium level of culture more than population size does, especially when connections are weak ([Kobayashi, Ohtsuki & Wakano 2016, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tpb.2016.07.001)). A single band of 20–40 people in which each learner effectively watches a few kin is the regime these models predict will lose complex skills. The "duller flake" is therefore an expected consequence of ACL's demography plus imperfect copying, not necessarily an implementation fault.

The theory is not settled, and ACL should not overclaim. Aoki shows that the confusion largely stems from a "mismatch between the theoretical variable and the empirical observable". A positive link between population size and toolkit size is predicted under many transmission modes, but *only* when population has been constant and the toolkit has reached equilibrium. Statistical tests on ethnographic hunter-gatherers find no such correlation ([Aoki 2018, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2017.0061)). Vaesen and colleagues argue that the Henrich and Powell models support a link between demography and culture "only … in implausible conditions" ([Vaesen et al. 2016, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1520288113)). The reply from Henrich and nine co-authors ([REC](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1610005113)) is remembered as arguing that the models concern the size and interconnectedness of the learning network, not census size, but that characterisation is unverified, as is the content of Vaesen et al.'s rejoinder ([REC](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1613074113)). Effective population size can also differ between traits within one population ([Premo 2016, ABS](https://doi.org/10.7183/0002-7316.81.4.605)). Whether demography matters at all depends on how complexity is defined ([Querbes, Vaesen & Houkes 2014, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0102543)). Group size matters for small groups but not larger ones ([Andersson & Törnberg 2016, ABS](https://doi.org/10.7183/0002-7316.81.3.576)). The treadmill model has become entrenched despite critiques ([Andersson & Read 2016, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1086/686317)). For ACL, the safe position is that the relevant variable is each skill's *effective learning pool*, and that it must be measured, not assumed.

### The freeze has four candidate mechanisms that leave different traces in the logs

Three model families can produce "novelty appears, then freezes". If the cost of acquiring a skill rises with its complexity, complexity hits an upper limit beyond which no innovation can occur ([Mesoudi 2011, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0018239)). When innovation is built from processes running at different rates, the result is punctuated stasis broken by sudden multi-trait gains or losses ([Kolodny, Creanza & Feldman 2015, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1520492112)). And small populations settle at treadmill equilibria (Henrich). The open-endedness literature adds a fourth reading. Most artificial systems belong to Bedau's "bounded" class: new activity spikes early and then falls to zero ([Bedau, Snyder & Packard 1998, REC](https://people.reed.edu/~mab/publications/index.html)). Each leaves a signature:

| Freeze type | What ACL's logs would show |
|---|---|
| Treadmill equilibrium | Stationary mean skill, with each generation's best-of-models gain just cancelling the per-copy loss |
| Acquisition-cost ceiling | Learning time per skill rising with complexity |
| Punctuated stasis | Long flat periods broken by multi-trait jumps |
| Bounded open-endedness | Rate of new persistent types falls to near zero while existing types stay in use |

### Connectivity helps most at intermediate levels, and only under some learning rules

The strongest result since 2016 is a trade-off. Full connectivity spreads the best trait but collapses diversity; heavy fragmentation generates variety but cannot hold onto complexity. In experiments, success-biased learning "drastically reduces cultural diversity within fully connected groups", while partially connected groups produced complex solutions "never produced in fully connected groups" ([Derex & Boyd 2016, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1518798113)). In an agent-based model, an intermediate level of fragmentation maximised cumulative cultural evolution ([Derex, Perreault & Boyd 2018, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2017.0062)). Migliano and colleagues ran a recombination model on the real Agta networks. The top-level innovation took **177 rounds** in the forest group's multi-camp network against **510 rounds** in a size-matched fully connected network, and 517 against 699 for the coastal group. As innovations grew more complex, the share contributed by non-kin pairs rose from 29% to 41% (forest) ([Migliano et al. 2020, FT](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aax5913)). Cantor and colleagues confirm that partially connected networks consistently beat fully connected ones on time to recombination. But modular structures also "make products more likely to go extinct", and which structure wins depends on population size, density and transmission mechanism ([Cantor et al. 2021, FT](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2020.3107)).

A 2026 preprint finds that under unbiased copying, network effects are weak except at very low connectivity. Under copy-the-successful, complexity rises with connectivity; under copy-successful-traits, it peaks at intermediate connectivity. It concludes that no single social structure always promotes cumulative culture ([Leite et al. 2026, PRE, not peer-reviewed](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.04.15.718734)).

Migration and turnover add specific predictions. In a model, one population of 50 holds about 40,000 tools at steady state, while the same population split into two isolated groups of 25 holds about 8,000. Even very rare migration after isolation can set off bursts of combinatorial innovation ([Creanza, Kolodny & Feldman 2017, FT](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2017.0171)). Dense networks raise proficiency but lose rare traits, while random links matter more than inherited ones because they shorten paths ([Smolla & Akçay 2019, FT](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aaw0609)). In 18 captive great-tit populations, gradually replacing members with naive immigrants "greatly increased the probability" that a more efficient behaviour replaced an established one, because newcomers sampled the better option ([Chimento, Alarcón-Nieto & Aplin 2021, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2021.03.057)). Limited connectivity between chimpanzee groups favoured the few cases of cumulative culture in that species ([Gunasekaram et al. 2024, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adk3381)). Central African hunter-gatherers descend from a deep history of partially connected populations ([Padilla-Iglesias et al. 2022, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2113936119)). Frequent local extinctions within a metapopulation suppress cumulative change and can mimic conformist transmission ([Premo & Kuhn 2010, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0015582)). That is a direct warning for ACL, whose worlds die out often. Intermediate environmental variability and mobility also raise the speed at which information spreads through forager networks ([Garg et al. 2021, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.211324)).

ACL currently has none of this structure. It has one band, almost no turnover and no immigrants, so it gets neither the maintenance benefit of a larger pool nor the diversity benefit of fragmentation. The multi-band world proposed for bottleneck 1 is therefore also the main structural treatment for bottleneck 2. The two bottlenecks share a cure, which is the strongest argument for doing it first.

### More models help only if learners can choose among them and keep what they learn

Fidelity and the number of models interact. Small gains in fidelity can switch a population from simple traditions to ratcheting, and combining traits matters more than inventing new ones ([Lewis & Laland 2012, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2012.0119)). Accuracy and the number of demonstrators jointly set the maximum complexity a population can sustain, with two regimes: "a few, simple traits" or "many, complex traits" ([Kempe, Lycett & Mesoudi 2014, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jtbi.2014.05.046)). In lab transmission chains, starting from trained experts, chains with one model lost knot-tying skill faster than chains with five models. In the final generation, every five-model participant outperformed the one-model participants ([Muthukrishna et al. 2014, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2013.2511)). In a computer game, larger groups kept knowledge "less deteriorated" ([Derex et al. 2013, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature12774)).

The evidence against is just as important. In paper-aeroplane chains (n = 543), only the one-model condition improved, by 1.21 m, and individual learners improved comparably, by 1.38 m. The extra variation from more models "may have overwhelmed their working memory" ([Fay et al. 2019, FT](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1811413116); also [Caldwell & Millen 2010, ABS](https://doi.org/10.3758/LB.38.3.310)). Children aged 5–10 could not fully combine information from two models displaced in space or time ([Wilks, Atkinson & Caldwell 2022, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1556/2055.2021.00005)). The decisive large experiment used 3,450 participants across 12 generations. Complex algorithms persisted when people could *choose whom to learn from*; without that choice they "frequently became extinct", and populations drifted to easy-to-transmit, lower-performing algorithms ([Thompson et al. 2022, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abn0915)). No source gives a universal "number of teachers needed"; the threshold depends on each skill's copying-error distribution.

### Suggestions for bottleneck 2 (transmission and demography)

All of these are measurements or seeded A/B experiments. None scripts an outcome:

- **Models-per-learner ledger.** For every agent and skill over a lifetime, log the distinct individuals observed performing the skill and the subset actually copied from (habit updates sourced from observation). Compare with Hill et al.'s order of magnitude of 300 toolmakers.
- **Fit α and β per skill.** Use the distribution of learner minus model proficiency. Combine with the measured number of models to predict Henrich's critical pool, and check whether the skills that decay are the ones predicted to decay. A skill that decays despite being predicted to grow points to an implementation artefact, such as habit values failing to transfer, rather than demography.
- **Test model selection.** Check whether the choice of whom to watch correlates with that person's payoff. Thompson et al. imply this is pivotal.
- **In-silico replications on seeded worlds**, with outcome metrics fixed in advance:
  - 1 versus 5 accessible models, via exposure radius or visiting (Muthukrishna);
  - immigrant turnover (Chimento);
  - selective versus random choice of model (Thompson);
  - travel cost varied across a range, testing whether accumulation peaks at intermediate contact (Derex 2018; Cantor 2021). Vary the cost, never the outcome.
- **Report the effective learning rule** (random, success-biased or kin-only) before interpreting any connectivity effect. Cantor and Leite show the sign of the effect depends on it.

## Watching must lead to practice before it becomes skill

### Forager children watch constantly but learn by doing

ACL lets agents learn by watching in only two narrow situations. Successful "planners" are respected but not copied, and useful techniques can sit unlearned in plain sight. The ethnography supports parts of this and contradicts others. Among Aka and Bofi children aged 4–18, observation took about **72 minutes a day**, against about 14 minutes of receiving instruction and 3 minutes of explicit imitation. Transmission is mostly from parents until age 4–5, then mostly from peers and other adults from about 5 to 12, and by about age 10 children know most subsistence skills ([Hewlett et al. 2011, FT](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2010.0373)). A meta-ethnography of 58 publications finds the sequence is watching, then play with toy or real tools in mixed-age playgroups, then participation. Several societies "actively refrain from instructing, directing, explaining, or correcting". Adolescents choose their own hunting mentors and seek out prestigious innovators. Ache men are reported to need **more than 35 years** to become proficient hunters ([Lew-Levy et al. 2017, FT](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12110-017-9302-2)). Across 40 sites and over 1,800 hunters, hunting returns peak at about **30–35** ([Koster et al. 2020, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aax9070)). BaYaka children learn visually transparent skills such as tool use by observation and practice, while teaching is reserved for abstract knowledge such as norms ([Salali et al. 2019, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-019-47515-8)). Most children's objects in 54 forager societies are miniature or full-size adult tools used for both play and work ([Lew-Levy et al. 2022, ABS](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.824983)). About half of children's learning from peers and other adults comes from non-relatives ([Hewlett et al. 2024, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2322883121)).

Teaching is real but a minority channel. Across 23 egalitarian forager societies it is tied more to values and kinship knowledge than to subsistence skills ([Garfield & Lew-Levy 2025, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-58764-9)). Its common forms are commands, negative feedback and giving access to tools ([Lew-Levy et al. 2017, FT](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12110-017-9302-2)). Child-to-child teaching is common ([Lew-Levy et al. 2020, search snippet plus record only](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2019.07.003)). For opaque, multi-step skills the case for fidelity is strongest. In Oldowan knapping transmission chains (N = 184), teaching and especially language improved transmission while imitation and emulation did not, and the authors link low fidelity to roughly 700,000 years of Oldowan stasis ([Morgan et al. 2015, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms7029)). (One note held this paper as record-only; the other read the abstract.)

### Copying the successful is the norm, but prestige is specific to a domain

The tournament evidence says copying pays because demonstrators filter: they perform what has already worked. The winning strategy learned almost entirely socially ([Rendell et al. 2010, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1184719)). People switch strategies flexibly with context ([Kendal et al. 2018, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2018.04.003)). Prestige is "freely conferred deference" that earns learners proximity to successful models ([Henrich & Gil-White 2001, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1090-5138(00)00071-4)). People use prestige mainly when direct success information is unavailable ([Brand et al. 2020, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-68982-4)), and they strongly prefer prestige earned in the *same* domain ([Brand, Mesoudi & Morgan 2021, FT](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0255346)). After a simulated migration, people relied more on conformity and on experienced locals ([Deffner, Kleinow & McElreath 2020, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.200734)). ACL's "respected but not copied" rule therefore runs against the main evidence, but two parts of it survive. Respect earned for planning need not transfer to toolmaking. And a plan is internal and cannot be seen, so "copy the habit, not the plan" is defensible. Generations can improve artefacts without understanding why they work, and passing on causal theories did not speed improvement ([Derex et al. 2019, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-019-0567-9)).

Over-imitation is robust across cultures, including Kalahari Bushman children ([Nielsen & Tomaselli 2010, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797610368808); [Nielsen et al. 2014, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12265); [Lyons, Young & Keil 2007, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0704452104)). But Aka forager children over-imitated least: 60% of successful Aka children emulated, against 0% of Western children ([Berl & Hewlett 2015, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0120180)). The zone of latent solutions offers a principled line. Many ape traditions are behaviours individuals could reinvent, spread by drawing attention to objects and places. Human ratcheting adds copying of process, teaching and conformity ([Tennie, Call & Tomasello 2009, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2009.0052); [Whiten et al. 2009, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2009.0069)).

### Reinforcement-learning results map directly onto ACL's habit learning

Two machine-learning results bear directly on ACL's Q-learned habits. DeepMind agents achieved robust real-time cultural transmission only with a specific combination: memory, attention to co-players, demonstrators who sometimes drop out, and a widening curriculum. Without dropout, agents only followed (score about 0.75) and lost the solution when the expert left ([Bhoopchand et al. 2023, FT via PMC structured extract](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10684502/)). The expert co-player in that study was hard-coded, which ACL must not copy. Across generations of reinforcement learners, "accumulation performance degrades when oracles are too accurate", while oracles that are too noisy prevent social learning altogether ([Cook et al. 2024, FT via HTML extract](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.00392v2)). Both point to the same diagnosis: if ACL juveniles copy only while a model is present, knowledge dies with its demonstrators. And if copying is either too faithful or too weak, the imitation/innovation balance sits in a corner where nothing accumulates.

### Suggestions for bottleneck 2 (social learning)

These widen *exposure*, not *transfer*:

- **Observation traces.** Any agent within sight of an action stores a weak trace of the form "object X used at place Y yielded food".
  - The trace only biases what the agent tries in its own exploration, especially when playing or idle.
  - It becomes a habit only after the agent's own successful repetitions.
  - This keeps "habit, not plan", keeps the zone-of-latent-solutions logic, and hands no agent an answer.
- **Age-structured learning as nature, anchored in the ethnography above:**
  - parents as the pool under about 5;
  - mixed-age peers and other adults from about 5 to 12, about half of them non-kin;
  - adolescents actively choosing skilled, accessible adults;
  - hunting proficiency rising into the 30s.
- **Prestige raises attention within its domain.** It increases the probability and duration of watching a model in that model's domain only, rather than copying a plan.
- **Copy more when uncertain.** Reliance on copying rises with the agent's own uncertainty and recent failures, and with recent arrival in a new area.
- **Tag each technique for transparency and number of hidden steps.**
  - For transparent, few-step techniques, observation mainly raises the odds of reinvention.
  - For opaque, multi-step techniques, observation transmits little unless exposure is repeated and close.
  - This anchors "useful techniques sit unlearned in plain sight" where the evidence supports it (Morgan 2015), and removes it where it does not.
- **A minimal teaching layer, carrying no content:**
  - tolerating a learner handling one's tool or joining one's task;
  - interrupting a learner's failing attempt.
- **Two measurements:**
  - the share of each skill's proficiency due to social versus individual updates. If it is near zero, there is nothing to accumulate culturally, and Fay et al. show individual trial-and-error can rival single-model copying;
  - a transmission score in the style of Bhoopchand: a juvenile's skill gain while an adult model is present versus after the model dies or leaves.

## A forgiving, stationary world gives tools nothing to do

### Open-ended systems keep changing the problem; rich but static worlds converge

ACL's observation that richer materials produced only more shallow play fits Banzhaf's typology. More materials add *variation*: new values of existing kinds of act. They do not add *innovation* (new kinds of entity) or *emergence* (new levels) ([Banzhaf et al. 2016, REC](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12064-016-0229-7)). The open-ended evolution field separates observable hallmarks from hypothesised mechanisms ([Taylor et al. 2016, ABS](https://research.aston.ac.uk/en/publications/open-ended-evolution-perspectives-from-the-oee-workshop-in-york/); [Packard et al. 2019, ABS](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.04430)). It also counts novelty only when it *persists because it is used*. That is the logic of Bedau's activity statistics and the MODES toolbox ([Dolson et al. 2019, ABS](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1162/artl_a_00280)), but MODES's four metric names and Bedau's neutral "shadow run" were stated from prior knowledge and need checking against the papers.

The machine-learning systems that sustain novelty all keep moving the target:

- **Competition.** Co-adapting hide-and-seek agents produced "multiple distinct rounds of emergent strategy" with tool use ([Baker et al. 2020, ABS](https://iclr.cc/virtual_2020/poster_SkxpxJBKwS.html)). The often-quoted count of six phases is from prior knowledge and unverified.
- **Co-evolving environments.** POET co-evolved environments and solutions, so solved problems became stepping stones ([Wang et al. 2019, ABS](https://qiniu.pattern.swarma.org/pdf/arxiv/1901.01753.pdf)). Enhanced POET's ANNECS measure is from prior knowledge ([Wang et al. 2020, REC](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v119/wang20l/wang20l.pdf)).
- **A conserved resource.** Flow-Lenia got richer evolutionary dynamics from a structural change, conserved mass and rules carried in the matter itself, not from adding kinds of thing ([Plantec et al. 2025, ABS](https://direct.mit.edu/artl/article/31/2/228/130572/Flow-Lenia-Emergent-Evolutionary-Dynamics-in-Mass)).

By contrast, a fixed tech tree like Craftax-Classic is "solved" to about 90% of optimum by plain PPO ([Matthews et al. 2024, ABS](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/matthews24a.html)).

The closest analogue to ACL, JaxLife (embodied neural agents with no LLM, aiming at open-ended culture across generations), reports only *rudimentary* communication, agriculture and tool use ([Lu et al. 2024, ABS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.00853)). The research notes found no non-LLM simulation from 2023–2026 with peer-reviewed evidence of long-run cumulative culture, after about 20 searches; that absence is weak evidence. So ACL's freeze is the open problem in the field, not an ACL-specific failure. LLM civilisation simulations ([Park et al. 2023, ABS](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3586183.3606763); [Project Sid 2024, ABS, company preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.00114)) start with human culture already learned in pretraining. A 2026 analysis of 800,000 posts on an agent-only platform found that **65% of comments shared no distinctive vocabulary with the parent post** and only about 5% took part in threaded conversation ([Shekkizhar & Earle 2026, PRE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20059)). The argument that LLM agents "remember rather than discover" is the notes' own inference from these sources; no single paper makes it, so ACL should present it as reasoning rather than a citation.

### Real forager environments are harsh through daily failure and lean seasons, not overall scarcity

The forager data say where harshness comes from. Daily hunting success is about **28% for the Hadza, 27% for the !Kung and 10–30% for the Efe**, and in Lewis et al.'s model enforced sharing protects individuals from shortfall on about two days in three. These values are secondary compilations ([Lewis et al. 2014, FT](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC4284614)). Over 2,076 hunter-days, Hadza took 71 large animals, just under 5 kg live weight per hunter-day ([Hawkes, O'Connell & Blurton Jones 2001, FT](https://in-africa.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/12/Hawkes-et-al-2011-EHB-Hadza-meat-sharing.pdf)). Binford's storage threshold is an effective temperature of **ET = 15.25 °C**, computed as ET = (18W − 10C)/((W − C) + 8) from the warmest and coldest monthly means. Below it, foragers must harvest a surplus to survive winter. Both the formula and the threshold come from secondary snippets ([Binford 1980/2001, REC](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/257578033_Exploring_Adaptive_Variation_among_Hunter-gatherers_with_Binford%27s_Frames_of_Reference)). Controlling for habitat quality, hunter-gatherers had less famine than farmers ([Berbesque et al. 2014, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsbl.2013.0853)). Resource stress predicts wider sharing across 96 societies ([Skoggard et al. 2020, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12110-020-09371-8)).

### Suggestions for bottleneck 2 (environment and novelty)

Tools matter little in ACL because nothing in nature makes them necessary. The evidence-anchored fixes all go into nature, and none specifies what to invent:

- **Daily failure.** Large, unpredictable packages such as game succeed on only about 25–30% of hunter-days.
- **Climate-derived seasons.** Assign each world a seasonal amplitude from a climate index such as ET, rather than tuning it. Worlds below 15.25 get a genuine season with near-zero fresh food.
- **Endogenous non-stationarity**, which makes yesterday's optimal habit sub-optimal and reopens search:
  - local depletion around camps;
  - population growth raising scarcity per head;
  - neighbouring bands competing for patches;
  - climate variability over decades to centuries.
- **A stepping-stone audit** of ACL's material-property system. Do products become inputs that open new affordances, as a container changes what can be carried and stored, or a cord what can be bound? If no product opens a new door, there is no ladder to climb.
- **Keep cheap exploration alive.** Novelty search shows stepping stones need not look useful when found ([Lehman & Stanley 2011, ABS](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/facultybib2010/1530/)). A curiosity or play drive anchored in human and animal evidence keeps low-payoff tinkering alive. A global novelty reward would script the outcome.
- **Knowledge carried in agents and artefacts.** Following Flow-Lenia's "rules in the matter", artefacts and marks that outlive their makers can act as external memory and be rediscovered.

For measurement, the notes propose a metric set assembled from several sources; it is a proposal, not a validated standard:

- **Technique registry with a persistence filter.** A type counts only after k users or T years. Track new persistent types, types alive, cumulative activity and loss rate per century.
- **Neutral shadow run.** Copying is random and techniques earn no payoff. If real persistence does not beat the shadow, it is drift.
- **Lineage depth.** Record which earlier products each item requires. Flat depth with rising counts is the "shallow play" signature.
- **Knock-out clones.** Remove all holders of a technique and record whether it is lost, retained by learners or reinvented.
- **Generational ratchet ratio.** Compare a cohort's skill with that of an isolated learner given the same lifetime experience ([Cook et al. 2024, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.00392v2)).
- **Novelty with learnability.** Check whether new behaviours are predictable from the existing repertoire ([Hughes et al. 2024, ABS](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/hughes24a.html)).
- **Freeze detector.** Record the environmental state at the onset of each freeze, to test whether freezes coincide with a stationary environment.

An external foundation model may serve only as an *observer* that scores novelty after the fact, as in ASAL ([Kumar et al. 2024, PRE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.17799)). Nothing inside the simulation changes.

## Storage fails in ACL for the reason it fails among mobile foragers

### Demand sharing removes the producer's return on surplus

ACL's third bottleneck reproduces real ethnography. On forest treks, Ache nuclear families gave away **80%** of what they acquired, and only 3.4% of family pairs never exchanged food. For non-meat packages over about 2,000 kcal, 70–90% was given away. Families kept about **1,500–2,000 kcal regardless of package size**, even for 20,000-kcal packages: "greater production does not mean greater consumption". On the agricultural reservation, 33% of family pairs had no transfers, and an item reached about 9% of families rather than 41% ([Gurven, Hill & Kaplan 2002, FT](https://gurven.anth.ucsb.edu/sites/secure.lsit.ucsb.edu.anth.d7_gurven/files/sitefiles/papers/gurvenetal2002JAR.pdf)). A successful Hadza hunter's household took only about **5–10%** of a large carcass ([Hawkes et al. 2001, FT](https://in-africa.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/12/Hawkes-et-al-2011-EHB-Hadza-meat-sharing.pdf)). Reciprocity, kinship and tolerated scrounging each explain sharing with similar effect sizes; for reciprocity, r = 0.20–0.48 ([Jaeggi & Gurven 2013, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2013.1615); [Jaeggi & Gurven 2013b, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1002/evan.21364)). Sharing is organised in levels, with clusters of 3–4 households inside camps ([Dyble et al. 2016, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2016.05.064)). A normative model reproduces this modularity as the structure that minimises starvation risk ([Plana, Pérez & Abeliuk 2023, FT, model not field data](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC10171659)). Generous sharers are provisioned by more people when sick ([Kaplan et al. 2000, FT](https://www.unm.edu/~jlancas/KaplanHillLancasterHurtado_2000_LHEvolution.pdf)). Across 22 societies in the Americas and Siberia, basic sharing practices showed no significant relationship to environment, except for dependence on animal husbandry and status distribution ([Ahedo et al. 2019, FT](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC6541262)).

The crucial mechanism is mobility. In Lewis et al.'s agent-based model, demand-sharing populations with free riders survive only when families can move between camps. Sedentary demand sharers accumulate free riders and go extinct, and "demand sharing does break apart when hunter-gatherer populations are forced to settle" ([Lewis et al. 2014, FT](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC4284614)). That model allows storage only as body fat capped at 30 days, an assumption based on hunger-strike data rather than forager field data. Large-scale storage among foragers is classically tied to four conditions meeting: abundant resources, seasonal resources, efficient mass harvest, and preservation. It also goes with sedentism, density and inequality. These statements come from secondary summaries; the paper's full text failed to load ([Testart 1982, REC](http://www.alaintestart.com/UK/documents/storage.pdf)).

### Suggestions for bottleneck 3

ACL must not script "this is mine". It should create the conditions in which restricted sharing became adaptive for real people, and then test whether it emerges:

- **Conditions in nature.** Worlds with ET below 15.25 and long zero-production seasons, mass-harvestable seasonal gluts, and preservation that genuinely slows spoilage.
- **Package properties in nature.** Size and predictability belong to the resource. How widely each is shared should emerge.
- **Capacities, not norms.** Producers can choose co-residents or leave, which is how Lewis et al.'s mobile sharers escape free riders and fits Dyble et al.'s co-residence choice.
- **A pre-registered test.** Does storage or sowing pay more for agents who can relocate or choose their co-residents than for those who cannot, with everything else equal?

The emergence tests, frozen in advance:

| Setting | Empirical target | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Mobile bands, large packages | about 70–90% given away; acquirer keeps about 5–10% | Gurven 2002 (FT); Hawkes 2001 (FT) |
| Mobile bands, food flows | about 80% of a family's acquisition flows out; about 97% of family pairs exchange food | Gurven 2002 (FT) |
| Sharing structure | clusters of about 3–4 households | Dyble 2016 (ABS) |
| Free riders | persist at about 10% of mobile populations (a model output, not field data) | Lewis 2014 (FT) |
| Under resource stress | sharing becomes wider | Skoggard 2020 (ABS) |
| After settling or farming | pairs with no transfers rise toward 33%; an item reaches about 9% rather than 41% of families | Gurven 2002 (FT) |

If ACL reproduces the forager pattern under mobility and the collapse under sedentism without either being coded, that is a strong emergence result in its own right. In that case, the failure of storage in a warm, mobile world is a *validation*, not a defect.

## Rigour: turn each emergence claim into a falsifiable card

ACL's existing toolkit already exceeds common practice. About 10% of 2,367 agent-based modelling papers from 1990–2014 shared code ([Janssen 2017, FT page summary](https://www.jasss.org/20/1/2.html)), and the notes found no published standard for pre-registering agent-based model experiments. The remaining gaps concern empirical patterns, circularity and effect sizes.

**Pattern-oriented modelling.** This tests a model against several independent empirical patterns at once, at different scales ([Grimm et al. 2005, REC](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1116681)). Agent-based model production has risen fast, but "the explicit use of POM has not increased" ([Gallagher et al. 2021, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1111/brv.12729)). The 2020 ODD update makes "Purpose and patterns" the first element and adds optional Rationale subsections ([Grimm et al. 2020, FT](https://www.jasss.org/23/2/7.html)). ACL's paper draft already has an ODD appendix. Upgrading it to ODD+D, which adds a section on decision-making and learning ([Müller et al. 2013, REC](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envsoft.2013.06.003)), with a Rationale citing the ethnographic anchor for each behavioural rule, would make "nature anchored in evidence" auditable. The emergence-test tables in this report are the start of a frozen POM pattern set. Some patterns should be held back as *holdout patterns*, never inspected during development, which is the empirical analogue of ACL's holdout seeds. Patterns should also adjudicate between rival submodels, as Carrignon and colleagues did when comparing three transmission hypotheses by Bayes factor ([Carrignon, Brughmans & Romanowska 2020, FT](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0240414)). A TRACE-structured modelling notebook ([Grimm et al. 2014, REC](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecolmodel.2014.01.018); [Ayllón et al. 2021, REC](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envsoft.2020.104932)) would give ACL's existing correction log a recognised structure.

**Emergence claims and circularity.** Generative models give how-possibly explanations, which are sufficient but not necessary ([Epstein 1999, REC](https://doi.org/10.1002/(SICI)1099-0526(199905/06)4:5%3C41::AID-CPLX9%3E3.0.CO;2-F); [Grüne-Yanoff 2009, REC](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11229-008-9429-0)). A confused modelling purpose is "a recipe for bad science", and hidden assumptions in code can undermine explanations ([Edmonds et al. 2019, FT](https://www.jasss.org/22/3/6.html)). Artefacts are outcomes caused by assumptions the modeller considers irrelevant, such as scheduling order or the shape of space ([Galán et al. 2017, REC; attribution via Edmonds et al.](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-66948-9_7)). ACL's sham arms test "no effect where none is expected", but they do not show a mechanism is *necessary*. Each claimed emergent phenomenon should therefore get a pre-registered card containing:

- the coded rules that could trivially produce it;
- an ablation arm that removes the mechanism;
- a scheduling and topology artefact test;
- an empirical pattern the model was not tuned on.

Read-only observers could also emulate how an ethnographer or archaeologist would sample the simulated society, so that comparisons with real data are like-for-like. This is the "virtual ecologist" approach ([Zurell et al. 2010, REC](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0706.2009.18284.x)).

**Statistics.** In simulations, p-values are set by replication, which can be arbitrarily high, and the null of no difference is known a priori to be false ([White et al. 2014, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0706.2013.01073.x)). For each frozen prediction, ACL should pre-register:

- a smallest effect size of interest and a primary effect-size metric, such as the median paired difference with a bootstrap interval, keeping the sign test secondary ([Lee et al. 2015, FT](https://www.jasss.org/18/4/4.html));
- a multiple-testing policy across the family of predictions;
- the Monte Carlo standard error of each headline number ([Siepe et al. 2024, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1037/met0000695)).

ACL's paired seeds act as common random numbers only if each stochastic process has its own random-number stream, so that one intervention does not desynchronise every later draw. The sham arms can be used to report the whole pipeline's empirical false-positive rate. Pre-registration of simulation studies is now recommended because questionable research practices can make a method with no real advantage look superior ([Pawel, Kook & Reeve 2024, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1002/bimj.202200091)). ADEMP-PreReg ([Siepe et al. 2024, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1037/met0000695)) and Crüwell & Evans's template for computational models ([2021, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.210155)) are adaptable templates.

**Sensitivity, calibration and replication.** 42% of highly cited sensitivity analyses fail to explore the input space properly ([Saltelli et al. 2019, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envsoft.2019.01.012)). Varying one factor at a time with 10 inputs covers about 0.25% of the space ([Lee et al. 2015, FT](https://www.jasss.org/18/4/4.html)). ACL should run Morris screening followed by Sobol indices on the outputs named in its frozen predictions. If ACL claims an empirical match, it should calibrate with approximate Bayesian computation and report coverage and which parameters cannot be identified ([van der Vaart et al. 2015, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecolmodel.2015.05.020); [McCulloch et al. 2022, ABS](https://www.jasss.org/25/2/1/1.pdf)). Re-implementing the same model on two platforms has produced different trends and conclusions ([Donkin et al. 2017, ABS](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envsoft.2017.01.020)), so an independent re-implementation of one critical submodule, such as reproduction or sharing, is worth its cost. ACL's repository already contains a Dockerfile. The remaining reproducibility steps are:

- pinning the random-number library version;
- checking reference-run state hashes in continuous integration;
- planning peer-reviewed archiving on CoMSES ([CoMSES](https://www.comses.net/)).

ACL's memory notes defer the Zenodo upload until 60% progress. An external timestamp on the *pre-registration files alone*, for example through OSF, would strengthen credibility without breaking that deferral.

**Frozen pre-registration and structural change.** The multi-band founding, new movement model and age-structured learning are changes to nature. They should be registered as a new model version with their own frozen predictions before any run. They should not be applied retroactively to experiments already registered. The old configuration should be kept as a reference arm, so that before-and-after effects are tested rather than asserted.

## Conclusion

The research changes the framing of ACL's problems more than it adds mechanisms. Partner scarcity, cultural decay and the failure of storage are not three engineering faults to patch inside a band. They are what demography, cumulative-culture theory and forager ethnography predict for an isolated band of 20–40 mobile demand-sharers living in a forgiving, stationary world. That is good news for ACL's scientific claims: reproducing these failures for the right reasons is itself a validation. And the remedies that are allowed under ACL's rules, such as a metapopulation, realistic movement, daily failure, climate-derived seasons and the capacity to move away, all come from measured nature rather than tuned outcomes.

The field also offers an honest ceiling. No non-LLM simulation has yet shown long-run cumulative culture, and the theory itself disputes whether size, connectivity or learner selectivity is the binding constraint. ACL's most valuable contribution may therefore be the measurement layer in priority 1. If ACL can say, for each skill, how large the effective learning pool was, how much each copy lost, and whether a freeze coincided with a stationary environment, it can adjudicate between Henrich, Fay and Thompson inside one controlled world. No lab experiment or ethnography can do that.
