THE ARTIFICIAL CIVILIZATION
The creator writes the laws of the universe, not the history.
The agents create the history.
Core Objective
Create a persistent artificial world containing autonomous beings that live, learn, reproduce, form relationships, experience changing internal states, discover uses for the world around them, create tools, exchange resources, develop cultures, and potentially build civilizations. The creator should define conditions rather than directly write outcomes.
The central constraint is emergence. No agent receives a predetermined life story. No civilization, culture, economy, technology tree, religion, government, nation, or other high-level social outcome is guaranteed. Agents can fail, die, lose knowledge, and disappear; different seeds should be capable of producing different histories.
This expanded draft does not replace the experimental discipline. It makes the existing rules more explicit so the coder, experimenter, and observer can interpret the same rule in the same way.
How to read this version
The book has two layers. First is the 27-chapter long-term architecture. Second is the existing numbered-law ledger inherited from the earlier World Bible. The law numbers are intentionally preserved so experiments and implementation notes remain traceable across versions.
The long-term architecture is a vision map, not a demand to implement everything immediately. The current MVP implementation focus is Chapters 1–8 of the 27-chapter architecture. Chapters 9–27 remain future layers that should be introduced only when experiments show the foundational world can support them without scripting outcomes.
A detailed rule contains four parts: Meaning, Operational interpretation, Emergence boundary, and—where useful—an Example / test case. The extra text explains the rule; it does not create an additional hidden outcome.

CHAPTER 1 — Universal Laws (Reality and Agent Foundations)
Status: FROZEN
LAW 1: Time Exists
Meaning
Simulation time is a real state variable. Events occur in an ordered sequence, agents age through that sequence, and causes must precede their effects in the world history. The observer may render time in a more convenient clock, but the underlying order remains part of the world.
Operational interpretation
Use a single authoritative simulation clock and a documented mapping between real time and simulated time. The current calendar uses 52 weeks of 7 days, with agents making decisions on the weekly schedule used by the current experiments.
Emergence boundary
Time may change the opportunities available to agents, but it must not automatically assign goals, progress, or civilization stages.
Example / test case
A storm occurring after a settlement is built cannot be recorded as if the settlement had been built in response to that future storm.
LAW 2: Time Is Irreversible
Meaning
The world keeps a forward causal history. Once an event has happened, later agents may remember, forget, misinterpret, or record it, but the physical event itself is not rewritten.
Operational interpretation
Advance world state monotonically and preserve event order in logs so replay can reconstruct the same history.
Emergence boundary
Irreversibility does not require perfect memory. History can be socially or individually misremembered even while the underlying world history remains fixed.
Example / test case
A murder in year 20 can be remembered for generations, while later generations can still blame the wrong person.
LAW 3: Space Exists
Meaning
Distance is part of the cost of living in the world. Agents must move through the world rather than teleporting between known locations.
Operational interpretation
Resolve movement tile by tile or through an equivalent path-consistent mechanism. Obstacles and terrain encountered along the route must matter.
Emergence boundary
Space must not be used as a hidden narrative gate. If two places are reachable under the world physics, agents can eventually attempt to reach them without a special story trigger.
Example / test case
A water gap blocks an agent without an appropriate floating capability because the route contains water; the path cannot simply be skipped.
LAW 4: Physical Objects Have State
Meaning
Objects are not labels alone. Their properties, location, condition, and relationships to other objects can change as agents interact with them or as nature acts on them.
Operational interpretation
Represent physical objects with persistent state and resolve transformations from properties plus actions rather than from named recipe lookups.
Emergence boundary
Object state should not secretly contain the answer to what the object is “for.” Usefulness is discovered through outcomes.
Example / test case
Two pieces of material can acquire a different combined state when combined, without the simulation checking a predefined “tool recipe.”
LAW 5: Energy Is Limited
Meaning
Useful action requires energy and energy is finite. Agents and organisms therefore face real tradeoffs between action, rest, feeding, movement, and other demands.
Operational interpretation
Track energy as part of the physical state and make major actions consume or alter it. Depletion can lead to weakness, reduced capability, or death according to the biological model.
Emergence boundary
Never use a universal reward that makes one activity valuable for all agents. Energy pressure creates constraints; agents still need to interpret circumstances.
Example / test case
A long journey can become impossible when an agent spends more energy moving than it can recover from the environment.
LAW 6: World Produces Resources
Meaning
The environment generates, contains, or renews resources through world processes rather than through unlimited inventory.
Operational interpretation
Resources should originate from places, organisms, weather, growth, transformation, or other world processes that can be measured and changed.
Emergence boundary
Resource production should not guarantee that a population finds a particular resource at the right time. Availability is a world condition, not a quest marker.
Example / test case
Rain can create temporary fresh-water opportunities, while seasonal changes can alter where food is available.
LAW 7: Resources Are Scarce
Meaning
Resources are finite relative to demand. Scarcity creates pressure, tradeoffs, competition, experimentation, specialization, and possible conflict without prescribing which of these outcomes will occur.
Operational interpretation
Give the world finite quantities, regeneration limits, spatial distribution, and consumption effects that agents can perceive imperfectly.
Emergence boundary
Do not force scarcity to produce markets or war. It simply makes resources matter.
Example / test case
A patch of edible plants can be depleted faster than it regenerates, changing the local options available to nearby agents.
LAW 8: Nature Changes
Meaning
Weather, seasons, terrain conditions, populations, and other natural variables change over time. Different places can experience different conditions.
Operational interpretation
Use changing environmental state such as wet/dry periods, storms, floods, hard winters, and local temperature conditions already used by the current experiments.
Emergence boundary
Nature must not act as a hidden storyteller. A change can have consequences, but the world should not create a specific “plot” response.
Example / test case
A flood may destroy an access route, alter food availability, or have little visible effect depending on what is located there.
LAW 9: Food Chains Exist
Meaning
Organisms exist within ecological relationships. Food is not an isolated inventory category: populations feed, compete, reproduce, migrate, and can affect one another.
Operational interpretation
Represent species or creature populations and ecological dependencies sufficiently for changes in one population to propagate through the environment.
Emergence boundary
The food chain must not force humans to eat a particular species or guarantee ecological balance. Extinction and population collapse remain possible.
Example / test case
A reduction in one prey population can reduce a predator population later, which can then change another population.
LAW 10: Environment Is Indifferent
Meaning
The world has no main character and does not protect agents because they are important. Survival depends on the same world conditions for everyone.
Operational interpretation
World processes continue regardless of individual importance. Failure, death, extinction, and empty regions are valid states.
Emergence boundary
Do not rescue a promising agent, settlement, invention, or lineage merely because it is interesting to the observer.
Example / test case
A whole founding population can disappear and remain valid experimental data.
LAW 11: Every Agent Is an Individual
Meaning
Each agent maintains its own state and history. Shared population-level statistics do not replace the individual differences that shape decisions.
Operational interpretation
Give agents persistent identity, local knowledge, memories, internal variables, and inherited traits where those systems exist.
Emergence boundary
Individuality does not mean every agent must be radically unique. Similar personalities can emerge from inheritance and environment without becoming scripted characters.
Example / test case
Two agents can experience the same event and later make different choices because their histories differ.
LAW 12: Limited Knowledge
Meaning
An agent does not automatically know the full world state. Knowledge must have a source: experience, observation, communication, record, inference, or inheritance of the limited kinds the system explicitly allows.
Operational interpretation
Partition world truth from agent-visible state. The agent receives only the information its sensors, memory, communication, or learned models can provide.
Emergence boundary
The observer may know the world state for measurement, but agent logic must not read the observer’s hidden state.
Example / test case
An agent can know where a nearby river is from experience while remaining ignorant of a distant island.
LAW 13: Imperfect Perception
Meaning
Agents can perceive incorrectly, incompletely, or with limited range and attention. What the observer renders as exact text is not automatically identical to what an agent “knows.”
Operational interpretation
Perception should produce an agent-level observation that can contain uncertainty, omission, or stale information. English speech bubbles and thought renderings are observer translations, not proof of an internal language.
Emergence boundary
Perception noise must not be a random excuse for arbitrary behavior; it should remain tied to the sensing limits and world conditions.
Example / test case
An agent may hear that water is nearby yet discover on arrival that the report was stale because the source meant water that was farther away.
LAW 14: Needs Exist
Meaning
Agents and organisms have needs that create pressure on decisions and connect behavior to survival and internal state. Current foundations include needs such as hunger, thirst, sleep, shelter, safety, social connection, reproduction, autonomy, and curiosity.
Operational interpretation
Represent needs as changing internal conditions affected by environment and action. Need satisfaction should come from actual world consequences, not free counters.
Emergence boundary
Needs are pressures, not scripts. A hungry agent does not receive a hard-coded “find food” command; it evaluates available options through its decision system.
Example / test case
An agent near an unfamiliar edible organism may investigate because hunger and uncertainty coexist, then learn from the result.
LAW 15: Drives Exist
Meaning
Agents can have persistent motivational tendencies such as survival, security, curiosity, social connection, status, reproduction, exploration, achievement, possession, or care.
Operational interpretation
Represent drives as individual tendencies that influence attention or action evaluation without directly selecting an action.
Emergence boundary
A drive must not force a universal behavior or guarantee a social institution. The same drive can express differently in different circumstances.
Example / test case
Two agents with strong social tendencies may still avoid one another if fear and past experience dominate the current context.
LAW 16: Personality Influences but Does Not Determine
Meaning
Inherited or persistent traits bias how an agent responds without turning its future into a fixed script. Existing inherited temperament traits include dimensions such as curiosity, sociability, persistence, anxiety, temper, boldness, kindness, and care.
Operational interpretation
Use traits as weighted influences on perception, memory, goal formation, or action selection where appropriate. Preserve room for learning and experience to change outcomes.
Emergence boundary
Do not map a trait directly to a guaranteed action, role, job, morality, or fate.
Example / test case
A bold agent may approach an unfamiliar creature more often than a cautious agent, but either can learn to avoid it after costly encounters.
LAW 17: Memory Is Selective
Meaning
Memory is not an exact database copy of the world. Agents retain some events, lose others, and interpret memories through their own experience.
Operational interpretation
Use bounded memory, importance or salience, abstraction, and forgetting mechanisms rather than unlimited perfect recall.
Emergence boundary
Memory errors can change social history, but the observer must retain separate world truth for measurement.
Example / test case
A remembered murder can persist across generations while details, names, or blame drift over time.
LAW 18: Agents Learn From Experience; Failure Is Information
Meaning
Actions produce outcomes that can update beliefs, skills, preferences, and future expectations. Failure is therefore a source of information rather than merely a penalty.
Operational interpretation
Log the relation between chosen action and observed result, and allow the agent’s later decisions to use those experiences.
Emergence boundary
Learning must not grant omniscience. One failed attempt can update a belief without revealing a complete causal model.
Example / test case
An agent that becomes sick after eating an unfamiliar organism may lower its confidence that the organism is safe, without immediately knowing the chemical reason.
LAW 19: Knowledge Is Shared Imperfectly
Meaning
Information can move between agents, but transmission can lose detail, distort meaning, arrive late, or be judged against the receiver’s existing trust and evidence.
Operational interpretation
Keep speaker knowledge, message content, receiver interpretation, and verified world outcome distinct.
Emergence boundary
Communication must not synchronize every agent into one shared global belief state.
Example / test case
A claim about an animal can spread as a story even after its original observation becomes uncertain or contested.
LAW 20: Communication, Emotion, Relationships, Family &amp; Generations Are Foundational
Meaning
Agents interact through communication and social relationships, experience derived internal states, reproduce, and belong to generational processes. These systems give individual histories continuity beyond a single lifetime.
Operational interpretation
Keep speech, emotional state, relationship dimensions, family links, births, deaths, and generational inheritance as separate but interacting systems.
Emergence boundary
None of these systems guarantees friendship, love, marriage, hierarchy, religion, government, or another specific social institution.
Example / test case
A parent can pass on behavior or temperament without passing on the exact knowledge that produced the behavior.
CHAPTER 2 — Emergence, Culture &amp; Civilization
Status: FROZEN
LAW 21: Principle of Emergence
Meaning
The creator supplies rules, constraints, resources, and agents; complex social structures must arise from interactions among those components rather than being inserted as finished outcomes.
Operational interpretation
Keep high-level structures outside the initial world state unless the experiment explicitly introduces them as conditions.
Emergence boundary
Do not pre-create governments, markets, religions, sports, professions, or nations merely to demonstrate that they exist.
Example / test case
If a market-like pattern appears because agents repeatedly exchange scarce goods, the pattern is an emergent result rather than a predefined “market” module.
LAW 22: Shared Meaning Is Assigned by Agents
Meaning
A symbol, practice, or object gains social meaning when agents come to associate it with something and that association affects later behavior.
Operational interpretation
Start marks or symbolic tokens without built-in meanings and detect repeated agent-created associations.
Emergence boundary
The world must not declare “this symbol means water.” Meaning is a social state built from agent behavior.
Example / test case
A repeated mark near a water source can eventually become useful as a location cue if agents consistently connect the mark with that place.
LAW 23: Symbol Evolution
Meaning
Meanings are not permanently fixed once discovered. They can broaden, narrow, split, merge, or drift as generations and contexts change.
Operational interpretation
Track symbolic associations over time and allow competing interpretations to coexist when the underlying evidence differs.
Emergence boundary
Symbol drift should not be programmed toward a desired language.
Example / test case
A mark first used for a particular shelter can later be treated as a general symbol for safe shelter if repeated use changes its social association.
LAW 24: False Beliefs Are Allowed
Meaning
Agents can hold beliefs that are incomplete, outdated, incorrect, or contradicted by later evidence. A simulated society should not be forced toward perfect rationality.
Operational interpretation
Maintain a distinction between belief state and world truth, with confidence and update mechanisms that respond to evidence.
Emergence boundary
False belief must not be injected simply to make stories interesting. It should arise naturally from limited evidence, communication, memory, and interpretation.
Example / test case
An agent may believe a certain animal is dangerous because of hearsay even after many direct encounters are harmless.
LAW 25: Rumors
Meaning
Information can spread beyond direct witnesses through social transmission, and each retelling can change fidelity.
Operational interpretation
Represent chains of transmission and keep source relationships distinct from verified truth.
Emergence boundary
Rumor generation should not assign a fixed “rumor count.” What becomes rumor depends on social pathways and repeated transmission.
Example / test case
A report of distant danger can spread through several agents and become stronger, weaker, or distorted before it reaches another group.
LAW 26: Myths &amp; Legends
Meaning
Stories can persist longer than the events that produced them and can accumulate social meaning, simplification, and distortion.
Operational interpretation
Allow narratives to be remembered and transmitted across generations without requiring the original event to remain physically observable.
Emergence boundary
A myth is not required to become religion or ideology. It can remain a local story, warning, memorial, or explanation.
Example / test case
A killing remembered 180 years later can preserve the existence of the event while the identity of the killer becomes increasingly uncertain.
LAW 27: Cultural Inheritance
Meaning
Behavioral patterns, symbols, stories, practices, and skills can persist socially between generations.
Operational interpretation
Permit imitation, teaching, observation, records, and repeated group practice to transfer patterns that are not encoded as genetic traits.
Emergence boundary
Cultural inheritance must compete with forgetting, death, isolation, and changing conditions.
Example / test case
A building practice can survive for many generations if successors repeatedly observe, imitate, and benefit from it.
LAW 28: Goal Generation
Meaning
Agents can generate goals from unresolved needs, surprises, opportunities, conflicts, or prediction errors rather than receiving a complete task list from the creator.
Operational interpretation
Translate salient internal or environmental events into candidate objectives for the agent’s own action-selection system.
Emergence boundary
Do not hard-code high-level goals such as “become a farmer” or “build a government.” The goal mechanism creates pressure; the specific goal is local to the agent.
Example / test case
Repeated surprise about where food disappears can produce investigation without the world ever naming “study the food chain.”
LAW 29: Invention Cycle
Meaning
Invention can arise through trying actions, combining materials or practices, observing consequences, and retaining successful procedures.
Operational interpretation
Provide primitive actions, properties, and a memory mechanism that can record useful sequences.
Emergence boundary
The cycle must not depend on a hidden recipe table.
Example / test case
An unfamiliar combination can become a reusable process only after an agent observes what happened and chooses to repeat it.
LAW 30: Scripts and Reusable Procedures
Meaning
Agents can preserve successful sequences as procedures or macros so that complex behaviors do not have to be rediscovered from scratch each time.
Operational interpretation
Represent procedures as combinations of lower-level actions that remain subject to ordinary world resolution.
Emergence boundary
A script is not a guarantee of success. Changing conditions can make an old procedure fail.
Example / test case
A repeated sequence for handling a material can later become a learned procedure, but it may fail when the material’s condition changes.
LAW 31: Technology Requires Preconditions
Meaning
Complex techniques require lower-level capabilities, materials, knowledge, and opportunities that make the technique possible.
Operational interpretation
Let the world’s action and property system naturally constrain what can be built.
Emergence boundary
Do not create a hard-coded technology tree. Preconditions arise from physics, materials, skills, and social transmission.
Example / test case
A deeper spear-like invention requires a suitable shaft, a point or cutting capability, and a process for combining them, rather than a “spear unlocked” flag.
LAW 32: Surplus
Meaning
Persistent surplus is a world condition in which production, storage, or resource efficiency can exceed immediate consumption, creating new room for optional activity.
Operational interpretation
Let surplus result from successful strategies, resource abundance, storage, reduced needs, or cooperation.
Emergence boundary
Surplus does not automatically create markets or leisure classes. Those are possible later responses.
Example / test case
If food can be stored safely, an agent may have more time available for experimentation or social activity than before.
LAW 33: Specialization
Meaning
Agents can become unevenly skilled or occupied because repeated practice, differing traits, local resources, social coordination, or opportunity make some activities more attractive than others.
Operational interpretation
Track learned competence or repeated practice without assigning fixed professions.
Emergence boundary
Do not create “farmer,” “smith,” or “merchant” roles as default categories. Roles must be discoverable social patterns.
Example / test case
A group may contain one person who becomes unusually skilled at making a certain object simply because they repeatedly practice the process.
LAW 34: Trade
Meaning
Agents can exchange resources, services, information, or access when both parties perceive the exchange as useful under their circumstances.
Operational interpretation
Permit giving and reciprocal or conditional exchange through primitive interaction capabilities.
Emergence boundary
Do not require currency, market prices, contracts, or money. Those are possible inventions, not prerequisites.
Example / test case
An agent with extra food may exchange it for a tool it values more, even without a named “trade” system.
LAW 35: Reputation
Meaning
Agents can carry forward judgments about others based on observed behavior, reported outcomes, and their own experience.
Operational interpretation
Maintain source-specific records that can influence trust separately from friendship or affection.
Emergence boundary
Reputation is not the same as a global score assigned by the world. It is relational and can differ between observers.
Example / test case
Someone can be trusted by one group and distrusted by another because their histories with that agent differ.
LAW 36: Norms
Meaning
Repeated social expectations can stabilize into patterns that influence behavior even when they are not physically enforced.
Operational interpretation
Detect repeated behavioral regularities and permit social consequences such as approval, rejection, teaching, or retaliation.
Emergence boundary
Do not create a fixed moral code. A norm only exists if agents collectively reproduce a pattern.
Example / test case
A group may develop a repeated expectation to share a certain resource after repeated cooperative encounters.
LAW 37: Institutions
Meaning
Durable coordination structures can form when repeated practices, records, roles, or authority relationships persist beyond individual encounters.
Operational interpretation
Allow stable patterns of coordination and information storage to persist across people and generations.
Emergence boundary
Do not spawn an institution because population reaches a threshold. The structure must be behaviorally supported.
Example / test case
A repeated process for resolving a resource dispute could become institutional if agents keep using and transmitting it.
LAW 38: Diversity
Meaning
Different groups and individuals can maintain different beliefs, practices, symbols, skills, and social patterns under different histories and environments.
Operational interpretation
Preserve local conditions and imperfect information so neighboring populations are not forced into immediate cultural convergence.
Emergence boundary
Diversity is not a guaranteed value or objective. Some cultures can disappear; others can persist or merge.
Example / test case
Two islands can develop different practices because their food sources, hazards, and contact histories differ.
LAW 39: Civilization Is a Process
Meaning
Civilization is not a switch or checklist. It is a changing pattern of population, knowledge, infrastructure, coordination, specialization, and accumulated history.
Operational interpretation
Measure civilization retrospectively through observable complexity rather than giving agents a “civilization level.”
Emergence boundary
Do not declare that a settlement becomes a civilization after reaching a numeric threshold.
Example / test case
A population may display advanced record keeping while remaining small and politically fragmented.
LAW 40: Cultural Detection
Meaning
Culture must be detected from persistent behavior, symbols, and transmission rather than assumed from agent labels or creator intent.
Operational interpretation
Observer tools should search for repeated patterns across agents and generations.
Emergence boundary
Detection belongs to the observer and must not change agent behavior.
Example / test case
A practice counts as cultural only when it persists and affects behavior beyond one isolated individual action.
LAW 41: Cultural Impact
Meaning
A cultural pattern matters when it changes what agents actually do, believe, remember, make, or coordinate.
Operational interpretation
Measure downstream behavioral effects rather than counting symbols alone.
Emergence boundary
A large number of marks without behavioral consequences is not automatically meaningful culture.
Example / test case
A shared mark becomes important when agents change travel or shelter decisions because they trust what it signals.
LAW 42: Open-Endedness
Meaning
The world should permit novel combinations and structures not listed in advance.
Operational interpretation
Keep system boundaries at primitive actions, constraints, properties, and observations rather than outcome catalogs.
Emergence boundary
Open-endedness does not mean infinite complexity immediately. Capability emerges as existing primitives interact and are recombined.
Example / test case
A new social practice can arise without there being a code path named after that practice.
LAW 43: Final Principle of Culture
Meaning
The creator simulates the conditions under which meaning and culture can emerge, not a finished culture.
Operational interpretation
Treat cultural mechanisms as enabling constraints and observer-detected patterns.
Emergence boundary
Do not convert cultural categories into mandatory world objects.
Example / test case
The simulator can detect a shared practice after it appears without having generated the practice beforehand.
CHAPTER 3 — Laws of Knowledge
Status: FROZEN
LAW 44: Knowledge Is Not Innate
Meaning
Agents begin without a complete catalog of world facts or techniques. Knowledge must come from permitted sources.
Operational interpretation
Initialize only genuinely required biological or architectural priors and keep learned information separate.
Emergence boundary
Instinct-like defaults must not secretly contain the world’s useful technology.
Example / test case
An agent should not begin knowing that an unfamiliar plant is edible merely because the code has marked it as food in a human-readable database.
LAW 45: Knowledge Is Personal and Local
Meaning
What one agent knows can differ from what another knows because exposure, travel, communication, and memory differ.
Operational interpretation
Attach knowledge or beliefs to agents or groups rather than maintaining a single global truth table for decision logic.
Emergence boundary
The observer can maintain global world truth for evaluation, but agents cannot use that hidden truth.
Example / test case
A hunter can know a safe route that a neighboring agent has never traveled.
LAW 46: Knowledge Has Cost
Meaning
Acquiring, verifying, storing, transmitting, or using knowledge consumes resources such as time, energy, attention, risk, or social effort.
Operational interpretation
Tie learning opportunities to actual experiences, attention, and the cost of investigation.
Emergence boundary
Do not give knowledge a universal fixed price. Cost depends on the world and agent.
Example / test case
Testing whether a distant water report is accurate requires travel and exposes the tester to whatever hazards exist on that route.
LAW 47: Sources of Knowledge
Meaning
Knowledge can originate from direct experience, experimentation, communication, inheritance of learned patterns, or inference.
Operational interpretation
Track provenance when practical so the observer can distinguish where a belief came from.
Emergence boundary
No source is automatically correct. Each source can be noisy or misleading.
Example / test case
An agent may learn that an animal is dangerous by being injured, seeing an injury, hearing a trusted report, or inferring danger from repeated evidence.
LAW 48: Forms of Knowledge
Meaning
Knowledge can be declarative, procedural, social, causal, normative, or symbolic, and different forms can behave differently.
Operational interpretation
Represent useful distinctions where the implementation can support them without forcing every belief into a single structure.
Emergence boundary
Do not assume one data structure captures all human-like knowledge. The observer can treat them as analytical categories.
Example / test case
Knowing “this place has water” differs from knowing “how to make the container that carries water.”
LAW 49: Knowledge Can Be Wrong
Meaning
A stored belief can disagree with world truth, sometimes for a long time.
Operational interpretation
Maintain separate world state and belief state and allow evidence to update confidence.
Emergence boundary
The system should not deliberately enforce a fixed percentage of false beliefs.
Example / test case
A local story about a dangerous animal can remain believed until contradictory evidence becomes stronger than the history supporting it.
LAW 50: Knowledge Is Fragile
Meaning
Knowledge can disappear when people die, memory fails, records decay, or transmission stops.
Operational interpretation
Tie knowledge persistence to actual carriers: minds, practices, records, objects, and institutions when they exist.
Emergence boundary
Do not preserve every discovered technique globally unless the world gives it a carrier.
Example / test case
A technique known by one person can vanish after that person dies if nobody observed, recorded, or copied it.
LAW 51: Knowledge Transmission Is Imperfect
Meaning
Teaching and communication transform information. Receivers may misunderstand, simplify, embellish, or reject what they hear.
Operational interpretation
Model the transmission chain separately from source truth and receiver belief.
Emergence boundary
Do not silently correct transmitted information to match the original source.
Example / test case
A repeated story can keep the event but alter the actor, location, or cause.
LAW 52: Knowledge Can Be Externalized
Meaning
Knowledge can leave the mind and persist in physical form through marks, objects, structures, or other records.
Operational interpretation
Permit durable records when agents have the capability to create them and let those records be interpreted through experience.
Emergence boundary
Externalization must not provide semantic meaning for free. A mark begins without guaranteed interpretation.
Example / test case
A durable mark can preserve a technique after the original maker dies if later agents learn how to connect it with the practice.
LAW 53: Specialization of Knowledge
Meaning
As knowledge accumulates, individuals or groups can know different subsets and become more capable in certain areas through repeated practice.
Operational interpretation
Allow uneven knowledge distribution and role-like specialization to arise through learning and opportunity.
Emergence boundary
No job titles are required. Specialization is a behavioral consequence of uneven knowledge.
Example / test case
One person may become the most reliable source for a particular material process because they repeatedly perform it.
LAW 54: Meta-Knowledge
Meaning
Agents can hold knowledge about the reliability, source, difficulty, or limits of other knowledge.
Operational interpretation
Allow beliefs to include provenance and confidence, not just content.
Emergence boundary
Meta-knowledge must remain local and can itself be wrong.
Example / test case
An agent can know that a certain person is usually right about water reports without knowing why that person is reliable.
LAW 55: Knowledge and Power
Meaning
Useful knowledge can change who can coordinate resources, predict events, perform valuable tasks, or influence others.
Operational interpretation
Let knowledge have consequences through the ordinary world rather than assigning it a direct “power” multiplier.
Emergence boundary
Knowledge need not always produce domination. It can also be shared, ignored, lost, or rendered obsolete.
Example / test case
Knowing a safe path during a storm can make one agent temporarily more valuable to a group.
LAW 56: Verification and Trust
Meaning
Agents can compare claims with outcomes and maintain records of whether sources have held in the past.
Operational interpretation
Separate friendship-based trust from track records of tested accuracy. Verified or disproved claims should update source credibility.
Emergence boundary
The world must not globally label someone “truthful.” Credibility is held by individual receivers.
Example / test case
A receiver may increase confidence in a source after several water reports are verified, even if the source is not a close friend.
LAW 57: Knowledge Accumulates
Meaning
Knowledge can build across generations when it is retained, transmitted, tested, and improved.
Operational interpretation
Allow successful practices to survive through memory, teaching, records, and repeated use.
Emergence boundary
Accumulation is not guaranteed. Isolation, forgetting, environmental change, and population loss can reverse it.
Example / test case
A simple process can become the starting point for a more complex invention if later agents understand and improve it.
LAW 58: Knowledge Frontier
Meaning
At any time, there can be things no living agent or group currently knows, including facts the observer can measure externally.
Operational interpretation
Keep the internal knowledge space smaller than the full world state.
Emergence boundary
Do not reveal the hidden frontier merely because the creator knows it exists.
Example / test case
A mountain, species, material property, or distant population can remain unknown until someone obtains evidence.
LAW 59: Knowledge Detection
Meaning
The observer can detect when a fact or technique has become widely known, specialized, lost, contested, or newly discovered.
Operational interpretation
Use read-only measurement across beliefs, practices, and transmission histories.
Emergence boundary
Detection must never write knowledge back into agents.
Example / test case
The observer can report that only two living agents know a process without making the process more likely to survive.
LAW 60: Knowledge Is a Living, Contested, Incomplete Process
Meaning
Knowledge is never a finished global database. It changes through experience, dispute, transmission, verification, and loss.
Operational interpretation
Design systems so belief states, evidence, records, and learned procedures can update over time.
Emergence boundary
Do not collapse knowledge into one authoritative truth store that agents can query.
Example / test case
A population can simultaneously contain a correct procedure, a false explanation for it, and several competing stories about its origin.
CHAPTER 4 — Laws of Discovery and Science
Status: FROZEN
LAW 61: Belief Is Not Knowledge
Meaning
A high-confidence belief is still a belief unless its support survives comparison with reality.
Operational interpretation
Keep belief/confidence separate from observer truth and expose the gap to experiments.
Emergence boundary
Do not award knowledge status because confidence is high or because many people repeat a claim.
Example / test case
Ten agents repeating the same untested rumor do not automatically create ten independent pieces of evidence.
LAW 62: Reality Is the Final Arbiter
Meaning
When a belief about the physical world can be tested, the world outcome decides whether the prediction held.
Operational interpretation
Use actual world outcomes as the reference for post-hoc accuracy measurement.
Emergence boundary
Reality should not be altered to preserve a popular belief.
Example / test case
If an agent predicts water at a location and repeatedly finds none, the prediction should count as failed evidence even if many people believed it.
LAW 63: Curiosity Creates an Opening
Meaning
Surprise, uncertainty, prediction error, or unexplained variation can create a reason for further investigation.
Operational interpretation
Allow unresolved surprises to influence attention or candidate goal generation.
Emergence boundary
Curiosity must not be a universal exploration reward. Its effect depends on agent traits, needs, emotions, memories, and circumstances.
Example / test case
An agent may investigate an unfamiliar organism because it is hungry and uncertain, while another ignores it because it is frightened or busy.
LAW 64: Experimentation
Meaning
Experimentation is deliberate action taken to learn about the world rather than only to achieve an immediate practical outcome.
Operational interpretation
Permit agents to try controlled or repeated actions and observe consequences.
Emergence boundary
Do not require a formal scientific method. Early experiments can be messy, accidental, or socially transmitted.
Example / test case
Trying different ways to process a material can reveal a property even before an agent knows why the successful method works.
LAW 65: Testing Requires Comparison
Meaning
A single surprising event is weak evidence. Repeated or comparative outcomes make it easier to distinguish a reliable pattern from chance.
Operational interpretation
Let agents compare outcomes across trials, locations, materials, people, or conditions.
Emergence boundary
The system should not force every agent to run identical experiments. Different agents can reach different confidence levels from different histories.
Example / test case
Testing a food item only once may be less informative than repeated outcomes across individuals and preparation methods.
LAW 66: Falsification Is More Powerful Than Confirmation
Meaning
Evidence that contradicts a prediction should strongly reduce confidence, while a confirming outcome need not prove a claim absolutely.
Operational interpretation
Update beliefs so failed predictions matter materially; current methodology emphasizes being proved wrong more than being proved right.
Emergence boundary
Do not hard-code certainty. A false positive or accidental success can still influence a belief if the agent interprets it as evidence.
Example / test case
One reliable counterexample can seriously weaken a general belief that a certain location always contains water.
LAW 67: Models Can Improve
Meaning
Agents can form simplified internal expectations about how things work and revise them after prediction errors.
Operational interpretation
Allow learned expectations or causal/procedural summaries to update with experience.
Emergence boundary
Do not require a single globally correct model or a perfect physics simulator inside every agent.
Example / test case
An agent can move from “this mark means water nearby” to “this mark often points toward water, but not always.”
LAW 68: Hidden Regularities
Meaning
The world can contain patterns not obvious from immediate observation. Some regularities may only appear after repeated experience or transmission.
Operational interpretation
Keep enough environmental structure for repeated patterns to exist without labeling them for agents.
Emergence boundary
Do not expose hidden variables as cheat codes.
Example / test case
Seasonal food availability can become predictable to agents that spend enough time observing it.
LAW 69: Discovery Can Be Individual or Collective
Meaning
Important discoveries can originate from one agent, a small group, or accumulated contributions across many people.
Operational interpretation
Let information and techniques move through social networks, records, and imitation.
Emergence boundary
Do not designate “the discoverer” in advance. The observer may detect first discovery after the fact.
Example / test case
One agent may notice a material property while another agent develops the first practical procedure based on it.
LAW 70: Social Error Correction
Meaning
Groups can improve belief accuracy when independent experiences, disagreements, testing, or records reveal contradictions.
Operational interpretation
Permit competing claims to coexist and allow social transmission of evidence and source records.
Emergence boundary
A group is not automatically wise. Social pressure can also preserve false beliefs.
Example / test case
Several agents can challenge a water report after independently failing to find the reported source.
LAW 71: Superstition Competes With Reliable Knowledge
Meaning
Explanations that feel socially satisfying can compete with explanations that predict reality well.
Operational interpretation
Allow beliefs to spread for social or emotional reasons while still permitting reality-based correction.
Emergence boundary
Do not create a predefined superstition system or force rationalism to win.
Example / test case
A ritual can persist because it is socially meaningful even after its supposed causal explanation repeatedly fails.
LAW 72: Reliable Knowledge Under Pressure
Meaning
Knowledge that survives consequences can become more valuable because failure carries real cost.
Operational interpretation
Let survival, resource loss, illness, or social consequences make some prediction errors more consequential than others.
Emergence boundary
Reliability is still contextual. A source can be accurate in one domain and poor in another.
Example / test case
A wrong warning about a dangerous animal can cost more than a wrong claim about a minor resource.
LAW 73: Discovery Cascades
Meaning
One discovery can create new questions, capabilities, resources, or experiments that increase the space of future discoveries.
Operational interpretation
Allow successful inventions and knowledge to change future options.
Emergence boundary
Do not prewrite the exact chain of discoveries. The cascade emerges from changed possibility space.
Example / test case
Learning to transport water can create new incentives to find containers, storage, or routes that were previously irrelevant.
LAW 74: External Records Strengthen Discovery
Meaning
Durable records can preserve discoveries beyond an individual lifetime and reduce dependence on memory alone.
Operational interpretation
Let records become observable sources with their own track records when agents learn to interpret them.
Emergence boundary
A record can still be misunderstood, damaged, or forgotten. External storage is not perfect truth.
Example / test case
A mark documenting a successful technique can let a later generation rediscover it after all original users have died.
LAW 75: Discovery Detection
Meaning
The observer can identify when a new causal regularity, technique, or reliable procedure appears in the world history.
Operational interpretation
Use read-only comparison across earlier and later world states and knowledge records.
Emergence boundary
Detection belongs to measurement; it does not announce discoveries to agents.
Example / test case
The observer can flag the first repeated successful use of a previously unknown process.
LAW 76: Truth Is Approached, Not Given
Meaning
Reliable knowledge is a moving frontier produced by repeated testing and correction, not a finished database handed to agents.
Operational interpretation
Allow uncertainty, revision, and partial models to persist.
Emergence boundary
The creator may know ground truth but must not use that knowledge to shortcut agent learning.
Example / test case
An agent can know a useful correlation without understanding its underlying mechanism.

Status: FROZEN

Meaning
Information is what is transmitted, stored, or exposed; knowledge is what an agent actually believes, understands, or can use.

Track messages and records separately from their effects on a receiver’s belief state.
Emergence boundary
A message does not become knowledge merely because it was delivered.

Information requires channels, attention, storage, transmission effort, or access.

Emergence boundary

Speech, demonstration, marks, messengers, rituals, records, and other media can carry information.

Represent channels with their own reach, bandwidth, latency, and fidelity characteristics.
Emergence boundary
The channel should not secretly guarantee understanding.

A face-to-face demonstration may transmit a procedure more clearly than a short verbal description.

Transmission can omit, distort, reorder, or reinterpret information.

Meaning
Physical records can preserve information beyond the life of an individual.
Operational interpretation
Let agents create and later encounter durable marks or other persistent information carriers.
Emergence boundary
Storage capacity and interpretation remain limited; a record is not an omniscient archive.
Example / test case
A mark can preserve a location cue after the person who created it dies.
LAW 82: Information Asymmetry Is Natural
Meaning
Different agents can hold different information because they occupy different places, have different experiences, or control different channels.

Allow unequal access to reports, records, locations, and observations.
Emergence boundary

Meaning

LAW 84: Trust Is Separate From Content

A receiver evaluates both what was said and who or what the source is believed to be.

Meaning
Access to timely, accurate, or rare information can have practical value when it changes decisions or outcomes.

Emergence boundary
Information need not always be valuable. Its value depends on what agents can do with it.

Knowing that a dangerous route is flooded may matter greatly before travel and very little afterward.
LAW 88: Bandwidth, Latency, and Noise Matter
Meaning
Channels differ in how much information they can carry, how quickly it arrives, and how likely it is to lose fidelity.

Emergence boundary
Do not optimize channels globally for realism if a simpler model already creates the intended conditions.

A local face-to-face report may be fast but short, while a durable mark may be slow to create but persist for years.
LAW 89: Information-System Detection
Meaning
The observer can detect stable communication networks, record systems, message bottlenecks, or channels that change social coordination.

Measure flows without injecting information into the world.
Emergence boundary
Detection must remain read-only.
Example / test case
The observer can identify that a particular location acts as an information hub because messages repeatedly pass through it.
LAW 90: Information Is the Circulatory System of Large Societies
Meaning
Large-scale coordination depends on information moving between people and places; without communication, complex coordination becomes difficult to sustain.
Operational interpretation

Emergence boundary

Example / test case

Meaning
No external controller should directly set an agent’s belief to force a desired social outcome. The receiver performs the final update.
Operational interpretation
Implement communication as an input to the receiver’s own belief and decision process. Keep observer state separate from agent state.

Emergence boundary

A clear claim from a trusted friend can still fail to persuade if the receiver has strong contradictory evidence.

Emergence boundary
This does not guarantee the invention of writing or assign a fixed semantic code. The association must develop through experience.
Example / test case
A mark associated with a useful technique can outlive its original maker and teach the procedure to someone who would otherwise have no living teacher.
EXPERIMENTAL EVIDENCE PRESERVED FROM v8.0
The expanded prose should not erase the evidence that motivated the later laws. The first runs remain part of the history of the rule book.
Culture can appear without scripting
In the recorded E1 runs, a building practice—heaping materials against the cold—persisted for about 190 years in 7 of 8 worlds and produced real dwellings. A killing in year 20 was still remembered 180 years later by 62 people across five generations, although later generations often blamed the wrong person. This supports the distinction between persistent cultural transmission and perfect historical accuracy.
Trust initially followed food more than verified accuracy
Food gifts caused most measured movement in trust, while tested claims were rare and had little relationship with source accuracy in the early design. The lesson was not to hard-code truth-based authority, but to create more opportunities for claims to be checked and for agents to retain source-specific records.
Belief can outlive practice
Sowing beliefs persisted after sowing behavior faded because the immediate payoff to the sower was weak. This demonstrates that social belief, personal behavior, and material payoff are separate variables and should not be collapsed into one “rational” state.
Knowing a use is not the same as using it
Rafts were invented quickly, but carrying them remained uncommon until the physical environment gave agents a felt reason to value floating. The rule-book response was to make real uses perceptible, not to reward raft use directly.
Small physics gaps can change history
Water crossings of up to three tiles could previously be completed by a jump-like movement check. That allowed many crossings without requiring floating capability and reduced isolation. Later movement handling made the path constraint physical rather than a single landing-tile check. The broader lesson is that small physical rules can have large social consequences.
Meaning can form and drift
Marks became shared symbols in the recorded worlds, often referring to places such as water, shelter, or graves, and meanings could drift over time. This is evidence for starting symbolic marks as meaningless rather than assigning a semantic dictionary to agents.
CREATOR METHOD
M1 — Predictions First
Before an experiment runs, write the predictions and freeze them. Afterward, score each prediction as confirmed, wrong, or unclear. This protects the experiment from being rewritten by hindsight.
M2 — Kill Criterion
Every experiment states in advance what result would show the tested idea failed or was unsupported. A mechanism survives by evidence, not enthusiasm.
M3 — Freeze During Experiments
Do not change the world rules while the experiment is running. Add only read-only measurement. A mid-run mechanic change makes causal interpretation unreliable.
M4 — Measurement Never Touches the World
Observers, historians, surprise logs, and metrics must not modify simulation state. A recorder is validated by comparing a run with the recorder against an otherwise identical run without it.
M5 — Many Worlds
One seed demonstrates one history. Use multiple deterministic seeds to distinguish robust patterns from accidents.
M6 — Law Accounting
Every implementation feature should identify the laws it supports and note where the implementation violates, approximates, or does not yet cover a law. This keeps the code honest about its relationship to the Bible.
M7 — Conditions, Not Outcomes
Before adding a major system, ask whether it creates a condition that agents can use, or whether it quietly scripts the social result. Conditions belong in the world; named outcomes should be earned through agent behavior.
M8 — The Bible Comes First
When the implementation requires a rule that the current Bible does not contain, document the departure and amend the next version before treating the new behavior as canonical.
M9 — One Observer Layer
The Surprise Log, historian, and experiment measurements should ultimately form one read-only observer layer rather than several competing measurement systems. Observers explain what happened; they do not participate in the history.
MVP IMPLEMENTATION BOUNDARY
The first implementation target is the foundational part of the 27-chapter architecture: Chapters 1–8. This means the world should first establish reality, physical constraints, environment, biology, ecology, and autonomous agents with needs, drives, perception, cognition, memory, learning, emotion, and individuality. High-level social systems should not be required to exist before the core can demonstrate stable individual and ecological behavior.
The runtime decision controller should remain lightweight and heuristic-driven. Formal statistics, causal models, mixed models, Bayesian analysis, and similar mathematics belong primarily in the observer and analysis layer unless an experiment explicitly demonstrates that a model is needed as an internal cognitive mechanism. Runtime core state should remain deterministic and discrete where practical; floats may be used for rendering or offline analysis, but must not silently alter core decisions.
The primitive-action boundary is equally important. The world should expose atomic capabilities grounded in the physical and biological systems. High-level outcomes such as farming, markets, governments, sports, religions, or modern science should not be atomic actions. Agents must have to assemble complex behavior from lower-level capabilities and learned procedures.
KNOWN GAPS THAT REMAIN OPEN
• Speech bubbles remain an English observer rendering; the agents do not yet have an emergent language system.
• Requests, warnings, plans, lies, and promises are named communication categories but do not yet have complete mechanisms.
• The exact long-term biology layer—body morphology, disease, aging, inherited physical traits, and richer population ecology—should be developed through experiments rather than assumed from chapter titles alone.
• The observer measurements and the unified Observer Layer still require implementation cleanup so that all measurement remains read-only and non-duplicative.
• The long-term chapters should be expanded only when the foundational experiments show that the underlying primitives are sufficient to support them without hard-coding outcomes.
FINAL STATUS
This document is an expanded draft, not a declaration that every chapter is already implemented. It preserves the long-term vision while making the current rules explicit enough for implementation, testing, and debate. The foundational chain remains: Reality → Culture → Knowledge → Discovery → Information → Influence → Power → Creation. The governing principle remains unchanged: the creator writes the laws of the universe, not the history.
Build the world. Start the clock. Let them live.
