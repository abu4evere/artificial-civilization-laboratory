## The Last Two

In the world grown from seed 1112, the last two children were born in years 49 and 51. Their mother, Thotham, was
forty-three when Kibok was born and forty-five when Riyei followed. After Riyei, no child was born in that world again.

There were eighteen people alive when Kibok was born. Kibok was sociable, more than most (0.77), curious, steady;
Riyei a little quicker to anger and a little less curious. They grew up, and grew old, and in all the weeks of their two
lives the replay keeps, more than three thousand three hundred each, neither of them ever had a partner, and courting
someone never once appeared among the choices either of them weighed. There was nobody to court. Everyone around them
was family or a generation away. They wished for someone all the same: as adults, three weeks in five, the thing on each
of their minds was *wishes for someone to be with*. Kibok carried it for about fifteen hundred weeks, and so did Riyei.

The world thinned around them. Sixteen people at year 60, twelve at 70, seven at 80, three at 90. By year 95 there were
two, a brother and a sister in their forties, and for nearly twenty years after that they were the whole of their
people.

They stayed together. From year 104 until Kibok's death they were within sight of each other more than nine weeks in
ten, usually two tiles apart. What held their attention is written down, week by week, by the part of the
code that reads a person's mind and does not change it. For Kibok, most weeks: *lonely*. Then *glad to be near Riyei*.
Then *hungry; where is food?*, and, season after season, *puzzling over what became of the wood left in spring*, the
wood they had set down somewhere and never found again. For Riyei nearly the same: *lonely*, *hungry*,
*glad to be near Kibok*, the same lost wood, and *cold; needs warmth*.

Kibok died of old age in year 113, at sixty-four. Riyei lived on alone for three and a half years, still gathering
plants, still setting things down. In her last recorded week she was sixty-five and her health had fallen to four; on her mind
were *feels ill*, *cold; needs warmth* and *hungry; where is food?*. She died of illness in year 116. The
world went on without anyone to see it.

Nothing killed that world. It was not hunger, though they were often hungry, and not violence or plague. It had simply
run out of people who could become parents, sixty-five years before its last death, and then it waited.

## The Worlds That Grew Old

It was not only seed 1112. When the creator stopped asking why ACL's people did not prepare for winter and asked
instead why so many of its worlds died, the answer was the same in every one of them. A third of the control worlds
died out, twenty-two of sixty-four, and in every one of the twenty-two the last child was born forty to sixty-five years
before the last death [F1]. The dying worlds were not hungrier than the living ones. Their land held as much food. Their
small children were always cared for. Their people simply could not find each other.

The reason was written into nature, though no one had meant it. A person looking for a partner in ACL courts only
someone in sight, four tiles away, or walks toward someone a little further, or else takes a few steps at random, on a
world of sixteen hundred tiles. In a small, scattered people a single woman almost never had an eligible man in sight,
though one was usually alive somewhere. By the twentieth year of a world, its size alone could tell whether it would
live, decades before the end.

The creator reopened what had been called the foundation of the project. Before anything else could be asked of these
worlds, they had to be able to go on.

## If Everyone Could Find Everyone

The evidence on real foragers offered two explanations that the worlds alone could not separate [N5]. Real people find
spouses in networks of hundreds, across many camps, mostly through marriages their kin arrange; ACL's twenty founders
were fewer than any estimate of a people that can last. So perhaps ACL's people could not find partners who were there.
Or perhaps there were too few of them for any way of searching to help.

The cleanest way to tell was to cheat on purpose, once, as a diagnostic and never as nature: let every single woman be
paired at once with any eligible man anywhere in the world [F2]. Seven worlds died instead of twenty-two. Perfect
finding saved seventeen of them, and peoples that had held still for a century began to grow. The seven that still died
told the rest: in every year a woman was single there, no man alive anywhere was neither her kin nor too far from her
age. The pool was not hidden; it was empty. The answer, by the rule frozen beforehand, was both, and it was written down
as it fell, one world above the line that would have said search alone.

## Apart Half the Time

Perfect finding showed something else. Once people could pair, their partners were so often out of reach. Watching
couples week by week, changing nothing, showed that ACL's couples had always spent about half their weeks apart [C1].
Each partner foraged wherever the food looked best, and nothing in either of them weighed staying near the other. The
wish to go back was there, among their first choices three weeks in four, and lost to food five times in six. Affection
was high. Hunger simply outbid it.

An earlier sentence had to be taken back here. The study before had said couples drifted apart once perfect finding
brought them together; counted properly, per couple, they had been apart all along. The correction was written beside
the old words, which stayed. The rule that corrections are added in the open, never edited away, had been in the
rulebook for a while; this was one of the times it was paid for.

Real foragers, the evidence said, are not always side by side either; husbands and wives often forage apart by day. But
they come back to the same camp at night, and the camp moves when the land around it is spent [N6]. ACL's couples had no
camp. Each person's "home" was only the place that had felt best lately, and partners' homes lay close in about half
their weeks [H1].

## A Home That Did Not Move

The small fix came first: two distances in the code disagreed, so that couples three tiles apart could neither conceive
nor feel any pull to close the gap. Aligning them changed almost nothing [T1]. The pull appeared and lost to food again.
That test taught the project something about itself: a change that barely moved anyone still changed the fate of
nineteen worlds in sixty-four, by chance alone. From then on, every test carried a sham, a set of worlds disturbed by a
single extra roll of the dice, so that chance could be seen beside the change.

Then the large thing: a shared home, the place both partners remembered as best, where they returned every week [HB1].
It worked exactly as intended. Couples were together almost every week. More children were conceived. And then the
worlds began to die: thirty-three of sixty-four against twenty-two, a difference the test said was not chance, while the
sham lost twenty-four. People were starving.

The obvious story was that families tied to one place had eaten the land around it. The creator would not build a
remedy on an explanation inferred from deaths, so the land was measured [HF1]. It was not bare, three per cent emptier
at most. The measuring itself had a fault the first time, a capacity read once and never updated as floods enriched the
soil; it was found, written down beside the numbers already seen, and fixed before any new worlds were run. Then the
children were blamed, and meat, and the gifts that pass from hand to hand [HF2, HF3]. Three predictions in a row were
wrong, and each was printed as wrong. The last look counted the food each person held at the moment of eating, inside the
world's own working [HF4]. Under a shared home it had fallen by about a fifteenth, a little for everyone, enough to push
many who were only just fed over the line. That explained about half of the extra hunger. The question was closed with
half an answer, and the half that was missing was written down too.

## A Camp That Moves, and How Many Worlds It Takes

The evidence had described a camp that people return to and that itself moves when the land around it gives less [N7].
HB1 had built only the first half. So a camp was built that went where the couple went: partners within a day's walk
ended each week together, on whichever of their two places held more food [MC1]. It did everything it was meant to.
Couples were together almost all the time, more children were born, and this time nobody went hungry for it. And the
worlds did not live longer.

Before giving up on it, the creator asked a question about the asking. The shams answered it: with sixty-four worlds,
chance alone moves the fates of a quarter of them, and a change would have to be large to show [N8]. So the camp was run
again, unchanged, on two hundred and fifty-six worlds that had never been run [MC1-L]. Couples stayed together, more
children were born, more of those children died, and ninety-three worlds died against eighty-seven. This time the null
had a size: at most, the camp could save about four worlds in a hundred. Staying together had been a limit on how many
children were born. It was not what decided whether a world lived.

## Nowhere to Go

The lever was still the one perfect finding had shown: people finding partners at all. The evidence allowed one gentle
form of it [N8]. In small groups, when there is no one, people leave to look elsewhere. The sources did not say how soon,
so nothing about *when* was touched. A single person with no one in sight, who already sometimes took a step to look
around, would take it outward instead of at random [P2].

A small run came first, to see whether the change would act at all. It barely did. In nineteen weeks of twenty a single
adult had no one eligible in sight; and in almost all of those weeks they foraged, fetched water, went home, sat by a
fire, stayed with their family. The step outward was taken four weeks in a hundred. The work stopped there, by its own
rule.

The brain scan, built later, shows what that looked like from inside one person. Vadumis, in seed 501, was thirty-six
and had no partner. Of the six hundred and eight habits an ACL person can learn, he had shaped seventy-eight in his life.
His strongest were giving food and gathering plants. His habit for courting, in every one of the thirty-two situations
the mind can be in, was close to nothing: a hundredth at most [brain record, seed 501]. Courting had never once been tried
and rewarded enough to leave a mark.

How much real people without a partner travel to find one, the sources agreed on direction and said nothing about size:
everyone visits, everyone moves, nobody has counted it [N9]. No number in ACL was changed on that.

## Months, Not Years

One thing the sources did agree on could be measured without changing anything: real forager bands do not keep their
people. Among the Ache, a band had the same members for months but not for years. In ACL, of adults who lived together,
seventy-eight in a hundred still did two years later, in the original worlds and in sixty-four new ones, almost to the
hundredth [GF1]. Groups simply stayed as they were, and the unpartnered stayed put like everyone else.

## One Band Alone

The creator looked at the world and felt it was too small for them. The evidence answered in people, not land [N10].
Across three hundred and thirty-nine foraging societies the same ladder appears: a family of four or five, a band of
about sixteen, a larger band of about fifty, a seasonal gathering of about a hundred and sixty, a people of about eight
hundred and forty. A model of how small a people can be and still not die out by bad luck puts the floor at about a
hundred and fifty, perhaps seventy-five. The two figures describe different things, and neither says how many founders
ACL should have; the creator insisted that be written down.

ACL began with twenty. Its whole world was one band, with no other band to marry into, visit, or move to. That was what
every study in this movement had found from its own side: the people could not find partners, did not go looking, and
stayed with the few they knew. It was why Kibok and Riyei, the last two children of their world, never had anyone to
court.

The world-size experiment, testing more land and more people, alone and together, against the world as it is, is
running as this is written [WS1]. The book stops here, as it promised to, until the evidence exists.
